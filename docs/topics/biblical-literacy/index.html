What Is Biblical Literacy?

Why do so many Christians read the Bible so little — and what would change if the church actually knew Scripture?

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is biblical literacy?

Biblical literacy is the ability to read, understand, and rightly apply the Bible: knowing what Scripture says, grasping the single story it tells from creation to new creation with Jesus Christ at its center, and having the skills to interpret a passage in its context. Bible literacy is not Bible trivia. A biblically literate Christian knows the text, understands what its authors meant, and is being shaped by it in belief and life (2 Timothy 3:16–17). The sections below define it, show why it matters, and gather the best evangelical guidance on how to become biblically literate.

TL;DR

Biblical literacy means knowing the Bible’s content, understanding its storyline and meaning, and letting it form you. Surveys show many Christians revere Scripture but rarely read it. Evangelical teachers agree the remedy is not a new program but old habits: reading whole books of the Bible, learning to ask what the author meant, sitting under faithful expository preaching, and reading Scripture together in the local church.

Ask a room of churchgoers whether the Bible is the Word of God and nearly every hand goes up. Ask them to trace the story from Abraham to David, to explain what a covenant is, or to say why Leviticus matters to a Christian, and the room goes quiet. That gap between reverence for Scripture and knowledge of Scripture is what evangelicals mean by the biblical literacy problem. It is not new: the prophets lamented a people destroyed “for lack of knowledge” (Hosea 4:6), and the writer of Hebrews rebuked believers who still needed milk when they should have been teachers (Hebrews 5:12). But a culture of skimming, scrolling, and verse-of-the-day devotionals has made it more acute.

The stakes are pastoral, not academic. Christians who do not know the Bible’s storyline struggle to read any one passage well, so they fall back on proof-texts, moralisms, or whatever a favorite teacher says. They are more exposed to the prosperity gospel and to progressive reinterpretations, and less able to weather honest doubt; many stories of faith deconstruction begin with a faith that was sincere but thinly rooted in Scripture. The good news is that biblical literacy is learnable. It grows the way all literacy grows: through steady reading, good teachers, and practice in asking the right questions of a text.

Evangelicals broadly agree on that diagnosis. Where they differ is in emphasis: whether the cure begins with training in careful study, with devotional habits that make the Bible a daily delight, or with the local church’s public reading and preaching of the Word. Most wise readers end up wanting all three.

Biblical literacy also sits at the center of several related questions. It is the fruit of the first of the spiritual disciplines, regular Bible intake. It rests on confidence that Scripture is trustworthy, the subject of biblical inerrancy. It equips believers to give reasons for their hope, the work of Christian apologetics. And it is one of the best safeguards against the drift into progressive Christianity that so often follows a sincere but thinly rooted faith. The companion guides after the FAQ take up each of these in turn.

By the Numbers

What Churchgoers Got Right

In August 2026 Lifeway Research quizzed 2,012 American Christian churchgoers on the Bible. Try five of the questions, reworded here, then see how churchgoers did on each skill.

  1. Who was the first king of Israel?

    Answer: Saul. Saul was anointed by Samuel (1 Samuel 10:1). David was the second king. 45% of churchgoers got this right.

  2. Which part of the Bible does 1 Samuel belong to?

    Answer: History. 1 Samuel is one of the Old Testament history books, between Ruth and 2 Samuel. 29% of churchgoers got this right.

  3. Is the Gospel of Thomas one of the books of the Bible?

    Answer: No. The Gospel of Thomas is not in the Old or New Testament. 56% of churchgoers got this right.

  4. Finish Micah 6:8: the Lord requires you to act justly, love mercy, and walk ___ with your God.

    Answer: humbly. "Walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). 28% of churchgoers got this right.

  5. "God helps those who help themselves." Is that line in the Bible?

    Answer: No. It is a proverb, not a verse of Scripture. 51% of churchgoers got this right.

The Evangelical Debate

Three Evangelical Paths to Biblical Literacy

Every voice below holds a high view of Scripture and agrees that the church has a Bible literacy problem. The disagreement is about where recovery begins: in the study, in the prayer closet, or in the pew. These are differences of emphasis, not of conviction, and most of these teachers would affirm all three.

Position 1
Systematic Study & Interpretation
Jen Wilkin · R.C. Sproul · Albert Mohler · Kathleen Nielson · Kevin DeYoung
This position emphasizes the importance of teaching people how to read Scripture skillfully, understanding grammar, historical context, and authorial intent. Advocates argue that biblical literacy requires disciplined study of the text itself, systematic engagement with Scripture's structure and flow, and developing interpretive skills to rightly divide God's Word. This approach stresses that Bible knowledge comes through careful, methodical study.

Position 2
Devotional Encounter & Transformation
John Piper · David Mathis · Tim Keller · Donald Whitney · Jen Wilkin
This position prioritizes personal, devotional engagement with Scripture as the pathway to spiritual transformation and biblical knowledge. Advocates contend that knowing Scripture is ultimately about encountering God and experiencing His presence, not merely accumulating information. This approach emphasizes meditation, reflection, and letting Scripture transform the heart and mind through the Holy Spirit's work, alongside whatever formal study occurs.

Position 3
Communal Interpretation & Church Formation
Mark Dever · Scot McKnight · Timothy Paul Jones · Jonathan Leeman · Kevin DeYoung
This position stresses that biblical literacy develops through the church community's gathered study, discussion, and interpretation of Scripture. Advocates argue that the Holy Spirit works through corporate worship, teaching, small groups, and pastoral ministry to build biblical understanding in believers. This approach sees Scripture reading and knowledge as inherently communal practices, where believers help one another understand and apply God's Word in context of local church community.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

Read side by side, these three emphases look less like rival positions and more like three strands of one rope. Careful study keeps devotional reading from drifting into “what this verse means to me.” Devotional delight keeps study from becoming a merely academic exercise. And the gathered church, where Scripture is read aloud, preached through book by book, and discussed together, is where both are tested and sustained. A Christian who has only one strand tends to fray.

The practical consensus is striking. Read whole books, not fragments. Ask first what the author intended, then how it applies. Learn the Bible’s big story so every passage has a home, and see how that story finds its center in Christ (Luke 24:27). Sit under preaching that explains the text, and read with others who will sharpen you. None of this is new, which is the point: biblical literacy is recovered less by innovation than by returning to the ordinary means God has always used to form his people through his Word.

Frequently Asked Questions

Biblical Literacy, Briefly Answered

What is biblical literacy?

Biblical literacy is the ability to read, understand, and rightly apply the Bible as a whole. It means knowing what Scripture contains (its books, people, events, and teaching), grasping the one storyline that runs from creation to new creation and centers on Jesus Christ, and having the basic skills to interpret a passage in its context. For evangelicals the goal is not trivia but transformation: knowing God through his Word and being shaped by it (2 Timothy 3:16–17).

What is Bible literacy, and is it different from biblical literacy?

The two phrases mean the same thing and are used interchangeably. “Bible literacy” is simply the more common everyday wording; “biblical literacy” is the term more often used by pastors, educators, and researchers. Both describe a working knowledge of the Bible’s content and story plus the ability to read it accurately for yourself.

How do evangelical teachers define biblical literacy?

Most evangelical definitions combine three things: content (knowing what the Bible says), comprehension (understanding what it means in context), and formation (being changed by it). Jen Wilkin offers a simple working definition in Let’s Talk about Our Bible Literacy Problem: having a Bible in a language you understand and “steadily moving toward knowledge and understanding of the text.” On that definition, literacy is a direction of growth, not a finish line.

What does it mean to be biblically literate?

A biblically literate Christian can find their way around the Bible, tell its big story in their own words, recognize how a passage fits that story, and ask the right first question of a text: what did the author intend? They read whole books rather than isolated verses, know the major covenants and how they point to Christ, and can test what they hear against Scripture, as the Bereans did (Acts 17:11). It is less about a score on a quiz than about being able to hear God speak accurately through his Word.

Why does biblical literacy matter?

Because Scripture is how God makes himself known and forms his people. Faith comes by hearing the word of Christ (Romans 10:17); Scripture equips believers “for every good work” (2 Timothy 3:16–17); and Jesus repeatedly asked his hearers, “Have you not read?” Christians who do not know the Bible are more easily swayed by false teaching, less able to give a reason for their hope (1 Peter 3:15), and more vulnerable when serious doubts arrive. That is one reason biblical literacy and faith deconstruction are so closely connected.

How can I become biblically literate?

Start with steady, whole-book reading rather than random verses: read a book of the Bible straight through, then read it again. Use simple tools such as a map, a timeline, a good study Bible, and a second translation. Learn the Bible’s overarching story so individual passages have a home. Sit under preaching that works through whole books, join a Bible study that reads the text closely, and memorize key passages. Twenty focused minutes a day, kept up for a year, will do more than an ambitious plan abandoned in February.

Is biblical literacy declining?

It depends on the survey and the measure. Barna reports weekly Bible reading rising in 2025, led by Gen Z and Millennials. American Bible Society reports that Scripture engagement came back down in 2026. Lifeway Research finds Gen Z churchgoers study the Bible more than older churchgoers, yet they are among the most likely to say it has not made a significant difference in how they live. Gallup finds fewer Americans than in past decades see the Bible as the literal word of God. Knowledge is a separate question: in Lifeway’s 2026 Bible literacy quiz, two in three churchgoers failed. Our survey tracker sets these surveys side by side, each with its own question and sample. As Albert Mohler argues in The Scandal of Biblical Illiteracy, evangelical leaders generally agree the problem is the church’s to fix through preaching, teaching, and family discipleship, not something to blame on secular culture.

How can churches improve biblical literacy?

Churches build biblical literacy by preaching through whole books of the Bible, reading Scripture aloud generously in worship, teaching members how to read (not just what to conclude), and centering small groups on the text itself. Equipping parents for family Bible reading, offering classes on the Bible’s storyline and basic interpretation, and modeling a love for Scripture from the pulpit all compound over time. Survey Tracker

Is Bible Reading Rising or Falling?

Engagement measures disagree, so the honest answer depends on the survey and the measure. Barna shows a 2025 rise in weekly Bible reading (30% in 2024 to 42% in 2025), led by Gen Z and Millennials. American Bible Society shows its Scripture Engaged segment down in 2026, at 17%. Lifeway finds Gen Z churchgoers reading more (22% study the Bible daily) but applying less (43% say it has not made a significant difference in how they live).

The Evangelical Conversation, Curated

To Foster Biblical Literacy, Teach People to Read
Benjamin Shaw · The Gospel Coalition
Shaw starts from The Atlantic's report on elite college students who have never read a whole book. He argues that biblical illiteracy is partly a reading problem: students trained to dissect stray paragraphs miss the clues that show what an author is doing across a whole book. To read Scripture well, Christians need to read a lot, starting with long books of every kind.
2
Let’s Talk about Our Bible Literacy Problem
Jen Wilkin · Crossway
Adapted from Wilkin's Women of the Word, this piece defines Bible literacy as having a Bible in a language you understand and “steadily moving toward knowledge and understanding of the text.” Wilkin argues that this growth does not happen by accident. Untrained, earnest readers settle into unhelpful habits, and those habits affect everyone they disciple.
3
How Do I Make the Most of Daily Bible Reading?
John Piper · Desiring God
In this Ask Pastor John episode, a listener asks which Bible-reading plan to use. Before recommending one, Piper says the right plan depends on a reader's discipline, available time, reading speed and comprehension, and curiosity. His first piece of advice is to belong to a church that faithfully preaches the whole Bible week in and week out.
4
Yes, Scripture Reading Really Does Change People
Terry Johnson · 9Marks
Johnson grew up in Bible-believing Southern California churches that almost never read Scripture aloud in worship apart from the sermon text. He contrasts that with the extended readings he heard in a conservative Anglican church in Bristol. He makes the case from Scripture's own testimony and the Westminster Confession for restoring substantial, systematic public reading of the Bible.
5
What Is Hermeneutics?
Jared Jeter · Ligonier Ministries
A four-minute primer on hermeneutics as both science and art: there are rules for interpretation, and wisdom is needed to know when to apply them. Jeter's central point is that the first question to ask of a passage is not “What does this mean to me?” but “What did the author intend?” Application comes after that.
6
Let Bible Reading Get Back to Basics
Jen Wilkin · Christianity Today
Wilkin warns that online lexicons, interlinears, and databases can push aside the simple tools that actually build comprehension. She recommends five: reading repetitively, consulting a map, keeping a Bible timeline, comparing translations, and checking an ordinary English dictionary. Each comes with a concrete example.
7
The Scandal of Biblical Illiteracy: It’s Our Problem
R. Albert Mohler Jr. · AlbertMohler.com
Mohler gathers the Gallup and Barna findings into a blunt diagnosis: Americans revere the Bible but do not read it. Fewer than half of adults can name the four Gospels, and most cannot list five of the Ten Commandments. He insists the problem is the church's own to fix through preaching, teaching, and family discipleship, not something to blame on secular culture.
8
How to Read the Bible Like the Early Church Did
Patrick Schreiner · Christianity Today
In an excerpt from The Four Senses of Scripture, Schreiner revisits Augustine's much-mocked allegory of the Good Samaritan, the usual example of how not to interpret. He asks what modern readers lose by dismissing the church's older ways of reading. It is a careful introduction to the literal, allegorical, moral, and anagogical senses for readers who know only the historical-grammatical method.
9
Reading the Bible with Ten Church Fathers: An Interview with Gerald Bray
Gerald Bray · Mere Orthodoxy
Bray, a research professor at Beeson Divinity School, explains how the Church Fathers interpreted, taught, and preached Scripture. He argues that their wisdom can make modern Christians more careful and engaged readers. It is a good next step for readers who want their Bible literacy to include the church's long history of reading.
10
Overcoming Fear and Perfectionism in Bible Reading (with Tara-Leigh Cobble)
Courtney Doctor & Melissa Kruger · The Gospel Coalition
Tara-Leigh Cobble, founder of The Bible Recap, describes how she got past her lack of desire to read Scripture and came to love its Author. She also describes what changes when someone gives just 20 minutes a day to Bible reading. The hosts and their guest recommend free tools for working through the hard parts of the Bible, which makes this a practical episode for readers stuck in guilt or perfectionism.
11
It’s Not Too Late to Make This a Great Year of Bible Reading
Tim Challies · Challies.com
Written for readers whose January Bible-reading plans fell apart by summer, this piece urges them not to wait for a new year to start again. Challies walks through how to read, what to read, and how to form a new habit or rebuild an old one. The goal is to redeem the months that remain.
