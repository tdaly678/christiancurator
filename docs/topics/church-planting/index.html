Best Christian Articles on Church Planting & Revitalization

The Great Commission requires churches to multiply — but what does faithful planting and renewal actually look like?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Church planting and church revitalization share a common conviction: that the local church is God's primary strategy for reaching the world with the gospel, and that where healthy churches do not exist, they must be established. One strategy multiplies by starting new congregations; the other by breathing new life into dying ones. Both are expressions of the Great Commission, and both require a robust theology of the local church as the foundation.

The evangelical church planting movement has grown significantly since the 1990s, producing a wealth of practical wisdom about what healthy church planting looks like — and, importantly, what it doesn't look like. Not every new church is a healthy church. The same theological convictions that mark a healthy established church — expository preaching, meaningful membership, church discipline, elder-led governance — are the convictions that should mark a new church plant from its first gathering.

Church revitalization is in some ways the harder calling. A church planter starts with a blank page; a revitalizer inherits an existing congregation with entrenched patterns, potentially unhealthy culture, and people who have been wounded by previous leadership. The articles below address both tasks with realism and pastoral wisdom.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What makes a church plant healthy from the beginning — and what are the most common failure modes?
  • What is the biblical basis for church revitalization? Is it worth investing in dying churches?
  • How does a pastor approach revitalization without destroying the congregation in the process?
  • What is the relationship between church planting and church revitalization — can they work together?
  • How should a sending church think about its role in supporting church plants?

The foundational theological case for why revitalization is a biblical burden, not merely a pragmatic strategy. This article argues that the New Testament's letters to existing, struggling churches — Corinth, Galatia, the churches of Revelation — are themselves evidence that God cares deeply about the health of established congregations and calls leaders to do the hard work of renewal.

13 Pillars for Planting Healthy Churches
· 9Marks
An essential checklist for church planters — not a procedural guide but a theological one. The thirteen pillars are convictions and commitments that a healthy church must have from the beginning: about Scripture, about the gospel, about membership, about preaching, about discipline. Churches that skip these foundations in the name of growth typically produce attendance, not formation.
3
Revitalization with an Eye Toward Church Planting
· 9Marks
A creative and compelling argument that revitalization and planting are not competing strategies but complementary ones. A revitalizing church that sends members to plant new congregations can be invigorated by the act of giving away — the outward missionary impulse itself becomes a catalyst for internal renewal. A vision for how revitalization and multiplication can work together.

Hard-won wisdom from the trenches. The unexpected lessons include how long genuine revitalization takes (longer than anyone wants), how much resistance comes from within, and why the pastor's own spiritual health is often the most critical variable. Honest, practical, and pastorally formed by real experience.

A balanced comparison of the two strategies, helping church leaders and aspiring pastors think through which calling they may be suited for. Both plant and revitalize aim to see a God-glorifying church established — but they require different gifts, different patience, and different expectations about the timeline to fruitfulness.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

Both church planting and revitalization are expressions of the same conviction: that the local church is the hope of the world, and that where it is absent or sick, something must be done. The Great Commission is not merely about individual conversions; it is about communities of disciples being established in every neighborhood, language, and culture — communities that will in turn make more disciples.

The wisdom emerging from this literature is sobering in the best sense: neither planting nor revitalizing is quick, glamorous, or guaranteed. But both are profoundly worth doing. A healthy church planted in an unreached neighborhood or a dying church brought back to gospel faithfulness represent some of the most significant kingdom work any pastor or congregation can undertake.
