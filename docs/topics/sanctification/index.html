Best Christian Articles on Sanctification

How do Christians actually grow in holiness — and what does the Bible say about the process?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Few questions are more practically urgent for the Christian than this: what does growth in holiness actually look like? Sanctification — the ongoing work of God in the believer's life after justification — sits at the intersection of doctrine and daily life. Get it wrong and you either collapse into legalism (try harder, be better) or license (grace covers everything, so nothing changes). Get it right and you begin to understand why the Christian life is simultaneously fought and received, effortful and free.

The evangelical conversation on sanctification has never been more nuanced. Reformed thinkers have pushed back against the Keswick "let go and let God" tradition, arguing that sanctification requires real moral effort empowered by the Spirit. Others emphasize definitive sanctification — the once-for-all break with sin's dominion at conversion — as the foundation that makes progressive growth possible. And debates continue about the proper role of the law in the sanctified life: does it function as a guide for believers, or primarily as a mirror that drives us back to the gospel?

What all serious evangelical treatments agree on is this: sanctification is entirely God's work, and entirely the believer's responsibility — a paradox that the New Testament sustains without resolution. Paul commands "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" in the same breath as "for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure" (Phil. 2:12–13). The articles below represent the best evangelical thinking on how to hold that tension together.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What is the difference between justification and sanctification?
  • How do definitive and progressive sanctification relate to each other?
  • What role does the Holy Spirit play in Christian growth?
  • How does the "mortification of sin" work practically?
  • What are the chief models of sanctification, and which is most biblical?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
Models of Sanctification
Andrew David Naselli · The Gospel Coalition
The essential map of the territory. Naselli surveys five major theological frameworks — Wesleyan, Keswick, Pentecostal, Chaferian, and Reformed — and explains why the Reformed model best coheres with the full witness of Scripture. Start here if you want to understand why evangelical Christians disagree about sanctification, and what the stakes of those disagreements actually are.
2
5 Things You Should Know About Sanctification
Nick Batzig · Ligonier Ministries
A concise and reliable doctrinal primer. Batzig walks through the Westminster Shorter Catechism's definition and builds outward: Christ as the source of sanctification, regeneration as its starting point, its definitive and progressive dimensions, faith working through love as its engine, and the Word and prayer as its ordained means. Dense with precision but written for ordinary readers.
3
Sanctification: So Why the Long Word?
Desiring God
An accessible entry point for readers encountering the term for the first time, grounded in John Piper's exposition of Romans 6. Why does the Bible use such technical vocabulary? Because imprecise language about holiness produces imprecise Christian living. This piece argues that the word itself carries the weight of the doctrine — and that recovering it recovers something urgent.
4
The Secret of Sanctification
Desiring God
A devotional-theological piece on the connection between the cross and holiness. The argument: every good thing in the Christian life — pardon, peace, holiness — flows from daily communion with the crucified and risen Christ. This is not a self-help strategy for personal improvement but a sustained meditation on why union with Christ is the only true foundation for moral transformation.
5
Is Sanctification the Pursuit of Perfection?
Desiring God
Tackles one of the most common pastoral misunderstandings: that if Christians are being sanctified, why do they still sin so much? This Q&A format article clarifies the difference between perfection as an eschatological goal versus a present-life expectation, and addresses how to persevere through the gap between who we are and who we are becoming.
6
A Christian Classic on Sanctification
Ligonier Ministries
An introduction to J.C. Ryle's Holiness — arguably the most important evangelical book written on sanctification in the past 150 years. This piece explains why a Victorian Anglican's pastoral theology remains indispensable for contemporary evangelicals: Ryle's insistence on the real moral struggle of the Christian life cuts against both cheap-grace antinomianism and moralistic self-improvement.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

Read together, these articles tell a coherent story: sanctification is neither passive nor self-generated. The Holy Spirit works through ordinary means — the Word, prayer, the Lord's Supper, and the fellowship of the local church — to form believers into the image of Christ. The models debate matters because wrong frameworks produce wrong expectations, and wrong expectations produce either despair ("I'm still sinning, so maybe I'm not saved") or complacency ("God will fix me eventually").

The Reformed tradition's contribution is to show that the same grace that justifies also sanctifies — and that the indicatives of the gospel (what God has already done) are the only stable foundation for the imperatives (what believers are now called to do). The fight for holiness is real; its power comes from outside us.
