What Is Faith Deconstruction?

When Christians begin questioning their beliefs, what does faithful deconstruction look like — and how should churches and pastors respond?

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is faith deconstruction?

Faith deconstruction is the process of critically re-examining the beliefs, practices, and assumptions of the Christianity a person inherited or was taught — taking faith apart to test what is truly biblical and what is merely cultural, tribal, or unexamined. Sometimes called spiritual deconstruction or the deconstruction of faith, deconstructing faith is not the same as abandoning it: for some the process ends in leaving Christianity, while for many others it leads to a rebuilt, more honest, and more deeply owned faith. The sections below trace how evangelicals define, debate, and pastor through it, with short answers to the most common questions about a deconstructed faith.

TL;DR

Faithful deconstruction involves honest questioning within a community committed to Scripture, distinguished from destructive doubt by its pursuit of deeper truth rather than abandonment of faith. Churches should respond with patient dialogue, creating safe spaces for questions while maintaining biblical foundations, recognizing that many deconstructing believers seek authenticity rather than rejection of Christ.

The term 'deconstruction' has entered evangelical consciousness with unusual urgency in recent years. Many evangelicals understand it as a dangerous process in which a believer systematically abandons Christian faith — a softening of conviction leading ultimately to apostasy. Yet some Christians, especially young evangelicals who grew up in fundamentalist or hyper-conservative contexts, describe deconstruction more charitably as a necessary process of reexamining inherited beliefs, questioning authority structures that proved abusive or false, and pursuing a more honest, less tribal faith. The different meanings signal a real pastoral and theological tension: How do we distinguish between healthy questioning and corrosive skepticism? When is deconstruction a work of the Spirit leading to deeper faith, and when is it a path away from Christ?

The deconstruction conversation reveals fault lines in contemporary evangelicalism: generational differences, unresolved trauma from authoritarian church cultures, questions about how to read Scripture in the modern world, and competing visions of what a resilient faith looks like. For pastors, it raises urgent questions: How do we create space for honest doubt without creating the impression that Christian faith is purely optional? How do we address past harms committed in the name of Jesus without destabilizing current believers' faith? How do we help young adults build a faith that can survive encounters with biblical scholarship, historical criticism, cultural challenge, and genuine moral questions the church has handled poorly?

Deconstruction rarely stays in one lane. Questions about the Bible soon become questions about biblical inerrancy; questions about evidence lead to Christian apologetics; and many people who leave conservative churches pass through progressive Christianity on the way somewhere else. What helps most is often less dramatic: growing Bible literacy and the ordinary spiritual disciplines of Scripture, prayer, and gathered worship that keep a doubting believer close to the means of grace. The companion guides after the FAQ take up each of those questions.

The Evangelical Debate

Three Frameworks for Understanding Faith Deconstruction

Evangelical thinkers have developed three distinct ways of understanding and responding to deconstruction, each rooted in different assessments of what deconstruction is, whether it can be redemptive, and what the church should do.

Position 1
Deconstruction as Apostasy
John MacArthur · Al Mohler · Voddie Baucham · Doug Wilson
Deconstruction, whatever its defenders claim, is a slippery slope toward apostasy. It begins with questioning secondary doctrines but inevitably leads to doubting core truths — the reliability of Scripture, the deity of Christ, the atonement. The social media culture of deconstruction rewards radical positioning and peer affirmation over truth. True Christians will have questions, but they will ask them within the boundaries of Scripture's authority and the church's wisdom. Deconstruction typically rejects both, replacing them with individual interpretation and cultural pressure. Churches should love deconstructing members but should not pretend deconstruction is spiritually neutral.

Position 2
Deconstruction as Necessary Reckoning
Amber Cantorna · Mike McHargue · Rhett Smith · Chrissy Stroop
For many evangelicals, deconstruction was necessary to escape spiritually abusive systems, fundamentalist inflexibility, and inherited beliefs held without honest examination. The question is not whether to question inherited faith but how and toward what end. Deconstruction becomes redemptive when it leads to genuine faith owned by the believer rather than inherited unexamined. Some deconstructing evangelicals do leave Christianity, but others reconstruct a more honest, humble, less tribal faith. The church's task is not to prevent deconstruction but to create safe spaces for honest questioning, to address its failures with repentance, and to show that biblical Christianity can survive scrutiny.

Faith Torn Down to the Studs
Position 3
Pastoring Through Deconstruction
Christena Cleveland · Paul Young · Brad Jersak · Rachel Held Evans (before her death)
Churches need not choose between defending doctrine and accompanying people through deconstruction. Healthy deconstruction is possible when believers are encouraged to ask hard questions within community, when church leaders show humility about historical failures and misinterpretations, and when the focus shifts from defending inherited systems to deepening love for Jesus. Many who deconstruct do so because received Christianity proved shallow or harmful. The pastoral response is not to shut down questions but to offer better theology, admit what evangelicalism got wrong, demonstrate how faith can be intellectually honest, and create cultures where doubt is not equated with betrayal.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The deconstruction debate reveals deep generational and institutional fractures in evangelicalism. Older leaders often experienced their faith being strengthened through challenge; younger evangelicals often experienced their faith being weaponized to justify abuse. Both experiences are real and both matter. The honest evangelical path forward is not to deny that deconstruction happens or that some deconstructing believers do leave the faith, but to ask hard questions about evangelical churches' failures and to create safer, more honest, more humble spaces where believers can question and grow without feeling coerced.

Perhaps the deepest evangelical consensus is that faith ought to be owned, not merely inherited. Parents can give children faith, but at some point adults must choose their own convictions. The question is whether that choosing process becomes destructive or constructive. Strong churches will create space for honest questions, model intellectual humility, admit past failures, and demonstrate that Christian faith can survive scrutiny and engagement with the real world. They will also maintain that deconstruction is not the same as reconstruction — that the goal is not endless questioning but deepening discipleship in Jesus. The goal of pastoring through deconstruction is not to prevent it but to guide it toward genuine faith.

Frequently Asked Questions

Faith Deconstruction, Briefly Answered

What is faith deconstruction?

Faith deconstruction is the process of critically examining the beliefs, practices, and assumptions a Christian has inherited, in order to test them against Scripture, experience, and honest questioning. It ranges from healthy re-examination that produces a stronger, personally owned faith to a more skeptical unraveling that ends in leaving Christianity altogether.

What does it mean to deconstruct your faith?

To deconstruct your faith means to ask hard questions about what you believe and why — separating what is genuinely biblical from what is merely cultural, political, or unexamined. For some the trigger is intellectual doubt; for others it is church hurt, hypocrisy, or abuse. The process itself is not the destination: it can move a person toward deeper conviction or away from faith entirely.

Is deconstruction the same as deconversion?

No. Deconversion means leaving Christianity; deconstruction means questioning and re-examining it, and the two do not always end in the same place. Some who deconstruct do eventually walk away, but many rebuild — a stage often called reconstruction — and arrive at a faith that is more honest and more resilient. Critics worry the process tilts toward unbelief; sympathetic voices insist the outcome depends on how, and with whom, the questioning is done.

Why are so many Christians deconstructing?

Common catalysts include spiritual abuse and church scandals, the entanglement of faith with politics, intellectual questions about Scripture and science, and disillusionment with shallow or tribal cultural Christianity. Social media has amplified the trend by giving deconstructing believers shared language, community, and visibility. Many are not trying to abandon Christ so much as to untangle him from things done in his name.

Can deconstruction be healthy?

Many evangelical pastors and writers believe it can be — when questions are pursued honestly, within community, and with Scripture kept on the table as an authority rather than taken off it. Healthy deconstruction distinguishes essential Christian doctrine from cultural add-ons and moves toward rebuilding rather than endless critique. Others avoid the word itself but affirm the underlying goal: a tested, personally owned faith instead of an unexamined inherited one.

How should churches respond to deconstruction?

With patience rather than panic. Pastors who walk with deconstructing believers counsel making room for honest questions without treating doubt as betrayal, acknowledging real failures in church culture, and keeping relationships open even when conclusions differ. The goal is not to shut the process down but to guide it toward reconstruction and deeper discipleship in Jesus.

What is spiritual deconstruction?

Spiritual deconstruction is another name for faith deconstruction: a season of taking apart inherited religious beliefs and practices to see which ones hold up. People use the phrase for everything from questioning a church’s culture or politics to doubting core doctrines such as the reliability of Scripture or the resurrection. Evangelical writers urge distinguishing between the two, because re-examining cultural add-ons is very different from setting aside the historic gospel.

Can a deconstructed faith be rebuilt?

Yes. Many Christians who have gone through deconstruction describe a reconstruction in which they return to Christ with a faith that is more honest, more rooted in Scripture, and less dependent on a particular church subculture. Rebuilding usually involves patient friends, a church that welcomes questions, and fresh, careful engagement with the Bible itself; growing in biblical literacy and in apologetics gives a rebuilt faith deeper foundations than the one that was taken apart.

The Evangelical Conversation, Curated

1
Before You Lose Your Faith: Deconstructing Doubt in the Church
Ivan Mesa, ed. · The Gospel Coalition
TGC's book-length answer to the “I'm deconstructing” posts that announce departures from the faith over sex, race, politics, science, or hell. Short chapters from pastors and writers, including Trevin Wax, Brett McCracken, and Jay Y. Kim, take those pressure points one at a time. It is a good first stop for a young adult who is wavering, or for a parent who wants to understand what that young adult is reading.
2
Doubt, Deconstruction, and Patient Faith
Jay Y. Kim · The Gospel Coalition
Kim went through doubt, deconstruction, and deconversion in college before returning to Christ, and he names what most deconstruction stories lack: patience. He uses the image of an intricate origami crease pattern for a faith that grows more complex with age, a faith to be worked through rather than crumpled up. He also points to Matthew 28:17, where “some doubted,” and notes that Jesus gave those same disciples the Great Commission.
3
Dealing with Doubt in an Age of Deconstruction
Paula Rinehart · The Gospel Coalition
Rinehart, a counselor, observes that in Christian circles doubt and disillusionment have become a new kind of enlightenment. In that climate, sharing doubts can sound more authentic than confessing faith. She argues the reverse: honestly faced, disillusionment can strip away false expectations of God and leave a sturdier, humbler faith behind.
4
The Best Advice for Dealing with Doubt Is Not to Ignore It
Rebecca McLaughlin · Crossway
McLaughlin calls doubt “a perfectly normal thing as part of Christian experience” and warns that trying to suppress it only leaves it hovering in our peripheral vision. Her counsel is to bring the doubt front and center and trace where it comes from. For doubts triggered by the church's sins, she points out that the New Testament itself leads us to expect a church full of sinners.
5
Faith Torn Down to the Studs
Jen Pollock Michel · Christianity Today
Michel opens with the night her high-school son told her that doubt had found him: questions about suffering, the Bible's reliability, and God's hiddenness. She uses that story to review Ian Harber's Walking Through Deconstruction. It is an honest parent's-eye account of how the “mythical land of certainty” gives way, and what it takes to rebuild a house of belief.
6
Help Stop Deconstruction. Become a Non-Anxious Presence.
Andrew Kelley · The Gospel Coalition
A pastor who has spent years discipling people through severe doubt reviews Ian Harber's Walking Through Deconstruction: How to Be a Companion in a Crisis of Faith (IVP, 2025). Kelley highlights Harber's two aims: to explain why deconstruction happens and to help friends and pastors lessen its intensity. The key to both is a calm, non-anxious presence, not a rush to win arguments.
7
Doubting Christians Are Jumping Out Attic Windows. They Don’t Have To.
Ian Harber · The Gospel Coalition
Harber reviews Joshua Chatraw and Jack Carson's Surprised by Doubt (Brazos, 2023). The book builds on C. S. Lewis's image of Christianity as a house with many rooms. Many people raised in a cramped, reactionary “attic” of the faith think the only exit is out the window. Chatraw and Carson show them the older, larger main floors of historic Christianity instead.
8
The Deconstruction of Christianity: What It Is, Why It’s Destructive, and How to Respond
Alisa Childers & Tim Barnett · Tyndale Elevate
The most direct critique on this list. Childers, a former member of the Christian band ZOEgirl, and apologist Tim Barnett define deconstruction and trace the assumptions behind it. They also give practical help to readers whose relationships have been strained by a loved one's deconstruction. TGC's Aaron Armstrong reviews it here.
9
How Progressive Christianity Preaches Another Gospel
Ian Harber · The Gospel Coalition
Harber reviews Childers's earlier book, Another Gospel?, written after a progressive pastor's class unsettled her faith. He follows her argument that the emergent church never disappeared. In this telling, it rebranded as progressive Christianity and became the destination for many deconstruction journeys. The review is useful for telling honest questioning apart from adopting a different gospel.
10
Editor’s Note: Defending Sound Doctrine Against the Deconstruction of American Evangelicalism
Jonathan Leeman · 9Marks
Leeman engages the deconstruction project at the level of ideas. He quotes David Gushee and other advocates who argue that evangelical doctrine is culturally conditioned and self-interested. He grants that every interpreter is limited, then contends that this does not dissolve the “sound doctrine” Paul tells Timothy and Titus to guard. A pastor-level case for holding the line on doctrine.
11
Hope for the Doubting and Deconstructing
Collin Hansen & Rebecca McLaughlin · The Gospel Coalition
This talk from TGC's 2024 Women's Conference takes on the questions that most often drive people out of church. How can you trust the Bible on sexuality when Christians have acted hatefully? How can you be a Christian given the church's record on race? Hansen and McLaughlin answer each by pointing to Jesus as the one who has the words of eternal life.
12
What Draws Me Back: 3 Arguments for the Faith on the Days I Doubt
Trevin Wax · The Gospel Coalition
Wax has no story of losing his faith, but he admits he still wrestles with doubt in what Charles Taylor calls a “cross-pressured” secular age. He describes three of his recurring doubts and the arguments that bring him back each time. It is a model of doubting without deconstructing: questions taken seriously inside a settled trust.
13
Deconstruction Has a Grief Problem Nobody Is Talking About
John Taylor · RELEVANT
Behind the public, triumphant face of deconstruction is a quieter one: people grieving the loss of a faith, a community, and a sense of home. Drawing on pastor, professor, and therapist Chuck DeGroat, the piece argues that the church has built almost no language for that loss. A helpful pastoral angle for anyone walking with a deconstructing friend.
14
Growing Number of Churchgoers Face Doubts
Aaron Earls · Lifeway Research
Data from Lifeway's State of Discipleship study shows that a growing share of churchgoers question God's character and involvement in their lives, even while most still trust his love and provision. As Scott McConnell puts it, churchgoers seem to be saying, “Sure, I trust God, except when I don't.” It is a sober baseline showing that doubt is common in ordinary pews, not only among online exvangelicals.
