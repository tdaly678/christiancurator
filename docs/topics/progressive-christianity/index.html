On Progressive Christianity

Is progressive Christianity a reform of the faith — or has it progressed so far it has become a different religion altogether?

A Christian Curator Topic Guide

Last updated: April 17, 2026

TL;DR

Most evangelical theologians argue progressive Christianity has departed so fundamentally from biblical authority, Christ's exclusivity, and historic doctrines that it constitutes a different faith system rather than legitimate reform. Some evangelicals see it as reviving social justice emphases, though nearly all identify irreconcilable differences on Scripture's authority and core theological claims.

Progressive Christianity represents one of the most significant departures from evangelical orthodoxy in contemporary religious life. It is not simply a liberal interpretation of Scripture; it is a comprehensive theological reorientation that challenges foundational evangelical commitments. Where evangelical Christianity affirms the authority of Scripture, the exclusivity of Christ's saving work, the reality of personal conversion, and the centrality of Christ's substitutionary atonement, progressive Christianity tends to emphasize cultural accommodation, religious pluralism, ethical concerns over doctrinal precision, and a Jesus whose primary work was social transformation rather than cosmic redemption. These are not marginal disagreements but fundamental shifts in how one understands the gospel itself.

For evangelical leaders and pastors, progressive Christianity represents both a cautionary tale and an urgent pastoral concern. Many who have deconstructed from evangelical faith have traveled through progressive theology on their way to skepticism or other faiths entirely. The movement's uncritical embrace of cultural trends—particularly on sexuality, gender, biblical authority, and the exclusivity of Christ—raises the question of whether it represents genuine reformation or systematic capitulation to secular culture. Yet the evangelical church's failure to adequately address questions about biblical interpretation, the treatment of LGBTQ+ persons, racial justice, and women's leadership has created a vacuum that progressive voices have rushed to fill. This suggests that addressing progressive Christianity cannot be merely defensive; it requires the church to recover a robustly biblical, intellectually rigorous, and pastorally winsome orthodoxy.

Key Questions

  • What precisely defines "progressive Christianity," and how does it differ fundamentally from historical evangelical faith?
  • On which doctrinal issues—biblical authority, the exclusivity of Christ, sexual ethics, the nature of atonement—does progressive Christianity most sharply diverge from historic Christianity?
  • How can evangelicals engage progressive Christians with intellectual honesty and pastoral charity while maintaining doctrinal clarity?
  • What drives young evangelicals toward faith deconstruction and progressive theology, and what catechetical failures does this expose in the church?
  • Is progressive Christianity a genuinely new movement, or is it merely liberal Protestantism re-branded for the contemporary moment?

The Debate

Three Evangelical Responses to Progressive Christianity

Evangelicals across the theological spectrum share concern about progressive Christianity's trajectory, yet they differ on the most faithful evangelical response: whether to refute it apologetically, engage it dialogically, or recover a deeper orthodoxy that transcends the evangelical-progressive divide.

Position A

Apologetic Refutation

Alisa Childers, Michael Brown

Progressive Christianity must be clearly identified as a departure from historic Christian orthodoxy and systematically refuted. It abandons the bedrock commitments of the faith—biblical authority, Christ's exclusive lordship, substitutionary atonement—in favor of cultural accommodation. The church's primary responsibility is to defend the faith once for all delivered to the saints, to expose the theological errors of progressivism, and to call people back to biblical Christianity. Dialogue without clear apologetics can inadvertently legitimize theological error.

Key Reads
Alisa Childers at The Gospel Coalition Michael Brown - Christian Post
Position B

Generous Dialogue

Tim Keller-influenced voices

Progressive Christians raise legitimate questions about how evangelicalism has sometimes accommodated to its own cultural captivity—tribalism, nationalism, materialism. Rather than dismissing progressivism wholesale, faithful evangelicals should listen carefully to the concerns driving it, engage progressive thinkers with genuine intellectual curiosity, and defend orthodoxy through winsome reasoning rather than polemic. This approach honors the image of God in progressive believers and creates space for genuine theological conversation across deep disagreement.

TGC: Questions Evangelicals Should Ask Talking Across the Divide (Crossway)
Position C

Prophetic Retrieval

Carl Trueman, Matthew Barrett

The problem is not that progressive Christianity has rejected evangelical faith but that it has rejected biblical, creedal orthodoxy itself. The solution is neither to defend evangelical distinctives nor to engage in undifferentiated dialogue, but to recover the deep catholicity of historic Christian doctrine—the ancient creeds, the theological wisdom of the church fathers, the Reformation's commitment to Scripture's authority. This retrieval of orthodoxy transcends the evangelical-progressive divide and grounds the church in something deeper and more enduring than contemporary culture.

Synthesis

Progressive Christianity reveals a crucial failure in evangelical catechesis and biblical formation. Many who deconstruct from evangelical faith do so not because they have encountered devastating intellectual arguments but because they have never been adequately taught why historic Christian doctrine matters, how to read Scripture with both faith and critical care, or how to think Christianly about complex cultural questions. The church's retreat into tribal identity politics on the right has been matched by its capitulation to cultural progressivism on the left—both represent a failure to ground the faith in the deeper wells of apostolic tradition and biblical authority. If the church is to respond faithfully to progressive Christianity, it cannot do so by defending evangelical distinctives or even by engaging in dialogue, though dialogue has its place. It must recover a deep, winsome, intellectually rigorous Christian orthodoxy that is both faithful to Scripture and capacious enough to address the legitimate questions progressive voices raise.

The most urgent pastoral task is not to polemicize against progressivism but to catechize the rising generation in the faith once delivered to the saints. This means teaching young Christians to read the Bible as a unified narrative of redemption, to understand the why behind Christian doctrine, to think carefully about how Scripture addresses cultural questions without simply capitulating to cultural pressure, and to recognize that the deepest human fulfillment comes not from aligning with the spirit of the age but from being conformed to Christ. Where progressive Christianity offers therapy and affirmation, historic Christianity offers truth, beauty, and a communion that transcends every human category. The church's witness in this moment depends not on defeating progressivism in argument but on embodying a faith so intellectually compelling, pastorally tender, and spiritually alive that it demonstrates the sufficiency of Christ and the authority of Scripture in a way that addresses the deepest longings of the human heart.
