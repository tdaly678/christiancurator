Creation Care

Is caring for the environment a Christian responsibility — or a distraction from the gospel?

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is creation care?

Creation care is the Christian conviction that God made the world good, gave human beings, made in his image, a charge to rule and keep it (Genesis 1:28 and 2:15), and holds them accountable for how they treat it. Evangelicals across the spectrum affirm the principle: the Southern Baptist Convention's 2006 resolution commits to "caring stewardship and wise dominion over the creation," and the Lausanne Movement's Cape Town Commitment (2010) says "creation care is thus a gospel issue within the Lordship of Christ."

The disagreement is over weight and method: how high creation care ranks beside evangelism, how much to trust climate science, and whether the answer runs through government policy, the church or private enterprise. See what the surveys show and short answers to common questions.

Also known as: environmental stewardship, stewardship of creation, Christian environmentalism, dominion and stewardship.

TL;DR

Most evangelicals agree Christians should care for creation as stewards of God's world, but disagree on priorities and methods. Some emphasize environmental activism as biblical obedience, while others caution against elevating creation care above evangelism or adopting secular environmental ideologies uncritically.

Creation Care is a significant topic in evangelical Christianity that touches on core convictions about faith, Scripture, and Christian practice. The evangelical conversation about Creation Care involves genuine theological disagreement among faithful Christians who share a commitment to biblical authority and the gospel of Jesus Christ.

What makes the conversation about Creation Care so important is its implications for how we live out our faith in the modern world. Different evangelical perspectives on this topic reflect different understandings of Scripture, tradition, and the application of biblical truth to contemporary challenges.

Understanding the evangelical debate over Creation Care requires careful attention to the biblical text, engagement with church history and theology, and a willingness to learn from Christians who interpret Scripture differently. The goal is not mere agreement but a deeper grasp of what Scripture teaches and how to apply it faithfully.

The Evangelical Debate

Three Evangelical Perspectives on Creation Care

Evangelical Christians affirm Scripture's authority, yet they interpret what it teaches about Creation Care in different ways. Here are three significant evangelical approaches to this important topic.

Position 1
Creation Care as Christian Obligation
Andrew Spencer · Russell Moore · Trillia Newbell · Sandra Richter · Lisa Sharon Harper
This position affirms that environmental stewardship is a core Christian responsibility rooted in the Creation mandate and our identity as image-bearers of God. Advocates argue that Christians should actively engage creation care as a matter of obedience to Scripture and neighbor-love, integrating environmental concern into discipleship and church witness.

Gospel Priority over Environmental Activism
John Piper · Tim Keller · Mark Dever · Kevin DeYoung · Voddie Baucham
This view holds that while Christians should exercise wise stewardship, environmentalism cannot become a substitute gospel priority. Advocates caution against allowing ecological concerns to eclipse gospel proclamation or worldly activism to replace gospel-centered ministry, arguing for balanced stewardship without idolizing creation care.

Position 3
Gospel-Rooted Stewardship
Scot McKnight · Kevin DeYoung · Cornelius Plantinga · Steven Bouma-Prediger · Wendell Berry
This position seeks biblical balance: affirming creation care as integral to gospel obedience while resisting environmentalism that eclipses Christ's lordship. Advocates argue that Christians should be distinctively excellent stewards grounded in Christ's authority over creation and love for neighbors, neither dismissing environmental concerns nor allowing activism to become idolatrous.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

These three approaches represent genuine evangelical engagement with Creation Care. All three are committed to Scripture, to the gospel, and to faithfulness in the church. What distinguishes them is how they interpret and apply biblical truth to this particular question.

The conversation about Creation Care ultimately reflects deeper convictions about Scripture, theology, and the Christian life. Engaging thoughtfully with different evangelical perspectives on this topic helps the church understand what Scripture teaches and how to live it out faithfully in our time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Creation Care, Briefly Answered

Is creation care a gospel issue?

Evangelicals answer differently. The Lausanne Movement's Cape Town Commitment (2010) says that "to proclaim the gospel that says 'Jesus is Lord' is to proclaim the gospel that includes the earth" and calls creation care "a gospel issue within the Lordship of Christ." The Southern Baptist Convention's 2006 resolution affirms "our God-given responsibility of caring for the creation" but warns that environmentalism could "distract its members from the priority of the Great Commission." Most evangelicals hold both convictions; the debate is over which comes first.

What do evangelicals believe about climate change?

In Pew Research Center's 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study, 91% of evangelical Protestants said God gave humans a duty to protect and care for the Earth, but 28% said the Earth is getting warmer mostly because of human activity, against 48% of all U.S. adults. In Pew's 2022 survey, 34% of evangelicals called climate change an extremely or very serious problem, the lowest of the major U.S. religious groups. See the chart.

Is the evangelical view of climate change about theology or politics?

Mostly politics, by Pew's measure. In its 2022 survey, 17% of evangelical Republicans and 78% of evangelical Democrats called climate change an extremely or very serious problem. Holding party and demographics constant, evangelicals were 5 points less likely than the religiously unaffiliated to call it a serious problem. Theology still shows up in the answers: 29% of all evangelicals said climate change is not a serious problem because God is in control of the climate. See the chart.

Do evangelical churches talk about creation care?

Not often. In Pew's 2022 survey, 60% of evangelical Protestants who attend services at least monthly said they had not heard climate change discussed in sermons at all. Among pastors, a 2019 Lifeway Research survey found 39% of evangelical pastors agreed that "global warming is real and man-made," against 71% of mainline pastors, and 47% of evangelical pastors said their church had taken tangible steps to reduce its carbon footprint.

Does belief in the end times make evangelicals less concerned about the environment?

Somewhat. In Pew's 2022 survey, 63% of evangelical Protestants said we are living in the end times. Americans who hold that belief were less likely than others to call climate change an extremely or very serious problem (51% against 62%), and those who also expect the world to decline until Jesus returns were the least concerned (40%). Pew calls the relationship modest.

What have evangelical bodies said about the environment?

The statements point in different directions. The National Association of Evangelicals, with World Relief, published "Loving the Least of These: Addressing a Changing Environment" in 2022, an update of a 2011 report, which opens by insisting that protecting the environment is a biblical mandate. The Southern Baptist Convention's 2006 resolution urges conservation but opposes "solutions based on questionable science." The interfaith Cornwall Declaration on Environmental Stewardship, now published by the Cornwall Alliance and written from "our common Judeo-Christian heritage," lists "fears of destructive manmade global warming" among "unfounded or undue concerns."
