Best Christian Articles on Christian Ethics

Christians agree that ethics must be grounded in God. How that grounding works — and what it demands of us in public life — is where the real arguments begin.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Christian ethics is not simply a matter of applying biblical rules to moral questions. It is a theological discipline that asks: what is the nature of moral reality, how do we know it, and how does the gospel reshape the way we engage it? Evangelical Christians have historically answered these questions in two main ways: through a doctrine of natural law that grounds moral knowledge in creation itself, accessible to all people through reason; and through a directly biblical ethics that derives moral norms primarily from Scripture. The tension between these approaches is one of the most productive in evangelical theology — and one of the most consequential for how Christians engage public life.

A second major fault line concerns the Christian's relationship to culture and society. The two-kingdoms tradition, associated with Lutheran and some Reformed thinkers, holds that Christians live in two spheres — the spiritual kingdom of the church and the civil kingdom of the state — governed by different principles, and that the church should not seek to impose a specifically Christian political or cultural program. The neo-Calvinist transformationist tradition, associated with Abraham Kuyper and Francis Schaeffer, holds that Christ is Lord over every sphere of life and that Christians are called to bring every domain — law, art, economics, education — under his lordship. These differences produce radically different visions of Christian political engagement, Christian education, and the relationship between evangelism and cultural renewal.

The articles here represent the best evangelical engagement with these foundational ethical questions — from the philosophical groundwork to the practical implications for vocation, politics, and moral formation. Christian ethics connects to the deepest questions of what human beings are and what we are made for — questions central to our coverage of gender and biblical anthropology.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What is natural law — and should evangelical Christians embrace it as a basis for public moral argument?
  • What is the difference between two-kingdoms theology and neo-Calvinist transformationism?
  • How should Christians engage in ethics in a pluralist democracy where others do not share their premises?
  • Is the Bible a sufficient guide for all ethical questions, or does Christian ethics need philosophy and natural law as well?
  • How do virtue ethics, deontology, and consequentialism map onto Christian moral theology?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
Christian Ethics
· The Gospel Coalition
The reference essay for evangelical Christian ethics — comprehensive and philosophically informed without being inaccessible. This piece lays out the major approaches to Christian moral reasoning, shows how the gospel reshapes ethical thinking at its foundations, and argues that Christian ethics is neither simple rule-following nor mere situation ethics but a theologically grounded account of human flourishing under God.
2
Two Kingdoms and Transformationism
· Ligonier Ministries
The clearest available comparison of the two dominant Protestant approaches to Christianity and culture. The piece explains the two-kingdoms position associated with David VanDrunen and Lutheran theology, the transformationist position associated with Kuyper and Schaeffer, and the practical differences between them for Christian political engagement, education, and cultural activism.
3
Natural Law and Evangelical Ethics
· First Things
A serious philosophical case for evangelical retrieval of natural law as a basis for public moral argument. The argument: in a pluralist democracy, Christians who argue only from Scripture have no traction in public debate; natural law provides a shared basis for moral reasoning that does not require biblical premises. This is one of the most important methodological questions in contemporary evangelical ethics.
4
What Does the Bible Say About Ethics?
· Crossway
Wayne Grudem's case for a robustly biblical approach to ethics — one that does not merely baptize natural law reasoning but derives moral norms directly from Scripture's own moral teaching. Grudem argues that the Bible is sufficient not just for personal morality but for informing Christian thinking about law, economics, and political philosophy, even in a pluralist context.
5
Why Christians Should Recover Virtue Ethics
· The Gospel Coalition
A case for virtue ethics as the most naturally Christian approach to moral philosophy — one that focuses not just on right action but on the formation of character. The argument: the New Testament's concern with the fruit of the Spirit, with becoming conformed to the image of Christ, is fundamentally about virtue formation, not mere rule compliance. This piece connects Christian ethics to discipleship and spiritual formation.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

Christian ethics is irreducibly theological: its distinctive contribution to moral philosophy is not a set of rules but a vision of reality — creation, fall, redemption, consummation — within which human moral life takes its meaning and direction. The debates about natural law vs. Scripture-alone ethics and about two kingdoms vs. transformationism are debates about how Christians should engage a world that does not share their premises. Both sets of debates are productive, and the best evangelical ethical thinking draws on multiple traditions.

What evangelical ethics must resist is two opposite temptations: the temptation to retreat from public engagement on the grounds that the church's only business is souls, and the temptation to identify Christian faithfulness with a particular political program. The gospel is not a political platform, and the church is not a lobbying organization. But Christians who are shaped by the gospel will inevitably have things to say about justice, human dignity, and the common good — and they should say them with both conviction and humility.

The Evangelical Debate
Two Kingdoms vs. Transformationism: How Much Should Christians Change Culture?

The most consequential debate in contemporary evangelical ethics concerns not a specific moral question but a fundamental question about Christianity's relationship to culture: are Christians called to transform every sphere of life under Christ's lordship, or is the church's calling primarily spiritual — leaving the civil order to operate by its own logic? This debate has enormous implications for Christian political engagement, education, and cultural activism.

Position One
Two-Kingdoms Theology
David VanDrunen · Michael Horton · Meredith Kline · Lutheran & some Reformed
Christians live in two distinct kingdoms: the spiritual kingdom of the church (grace, gospel, Word and sacrament) and the civil kingdom of the state (creation, natural law, common grace). These operate by different principles and must not be confused. The church's task is proclamation and discipleship, not cultural transformation. Christians should engage culture as citizens, not as agents of Christianization — using natural law, not biblical law, as the basis for public moral argument.

Position Two
Neo-Calvinist Transformationism
Abraham Kuyper · Francis Schaeffer · Tim Keller · Wayne Grudem
"There is not a square inch in the whole domain of human existence over which Christ does not cry: Mine!" (Kuyper). Christ's lordship extends over every sphere — art, education, economics, law, politics — and Christians are called to bring these spheres under his rule. The gospel is not just personal redemption but cosmic renewal, and Christian cultural engagement is an act of kingdom building. The church should shape culture, not merely occupy a spiritual enclave within it.
