Spiritual Disciplines and Christian Formation

Prayer, fasting, Scripture, sabbath — the classical practices of Christian formation and why they remain essential to growing in grace.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

The spiritual disciplines are among the most misunderstood features of the Christian life. In some traditions, they have become the currency of a works-based spirituality — as though prayer, fasting, and Scripture reading earn God’s favor or signal advanced spiritual status. In reaction, other traditions have abandoned structured practice altogether in favor of spontaneity, treating any regularity as legalism. Both errors impoverish the Christian. The disciplines are neither meritorious works nor optional additions to a minimalist faith. They are, as Dallas Willard famously put it, training for godliness — practices that do not produce grace but position the soul to receive — what our guide on sanctification calls the fight for holiness what God freely gives.

The classical disciplines — chief among them prayer — — prayer, fasting, Bible reading, sabbath, solitude, confession, worship — have structured Christian formation for two thousand years. They appear throughout the Scriptures not as commands to perform but as patterns to inhabit. Jesus fasted, prayed in solitude, observed the sabbath, and immersed himself in the Psalms. The apostles devoted themselves to prayer and the word. The monastic tradition, whatever its excesses, recognized something true: that the soul is shaped by what it repeatedly does, and that without intentional practice, the Christian will be formed by the world rather than by Christ. The evangelical recovery of spiritual disciplines in the past century — through writers like Richard Foster, Dallas Willard, and Donald Whitney — has restored this wisdom to Protestant churches that had largely abandoned it.

The question for contemporary evangelicals is not whether to practice disciplines but how to practice them in a gospel-centered way. The disciplines must be understood as responses to grace rather than means of earning it — and this distinction changes everything about how they are taught and practiced. A Christian who fasts to impress God or accumulate spiritual merit has misunderstood the gospel. But a Christian who fasts because she is desperate for God, who reads Scripture because she wants to hear her Father speak, who keeps sabbath because she trusts God to sustain the world without her — that Christian is practicing disciplines as they are meant to be practiced: as acts of faith and dependence, not performance.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What are spiritual disciplines, and how do they relate to grace rather than works?
  • Which disciplines are most foundational, and how should ordinary Christians prioritize them?
  • What is the biblical basis for fasting, and what is it actually for?
  • How does sabbath work as a spiritual discipline in a modern context?
  • How should churches teach and practice disciplines without falling into legalism?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
What Are Spiritual Disciplines?
· Desiring God
The best short answer to the foundational question: what are spiritual disciplines, why do they matter, and what is their relationship to grace? Establishes the key insight that disciplines are not ways of earning God’s favor but means of positioning yourself to receive what he freely gives — a distinction that keeps spiritual formation in its proper gospel context.
2
Fasting Isn’t for the Spiritually Elite. It’s for the Hurting.
· The Gospel Coalition
Reframes fasting as an ordinary practice for ordinary Christians in seasons of need, loss, or seeking — not a spiritual achievement for the advanced. Draws on the biblical pattern of fasting as a response to grief, urgency, and longing that intensifies prayer by embodying it. One of the most practically liberating pieces written on this discipline in recent years.
3
How to Read the Bible for Yourself
· The Gospel Coalition
A practical guide to personal Bible reading as a spiritual discipline — covering how to approach different genres, how to read for transformation rather than information, and how to build sustainable habits. Essential for churches wanting to cultivate biblical literacy in their congregations and for individuals who want their Scripture reading to actually shape them.
4
The Discipline of Sabbath
· Crossway
The most theologically rich and practically useful treatment of sabbath as a spiritual discipline. Traces sabbath from creation through Israel through Christ’s fulfillment and into the Christian practice of weekly rest — showing why stopping is not laziness but a declaration of dependence on God and a foretaste of the eternal rest that awaits his people.
5
Prayer as a Spiritual Discipline
· Ligonier Ministries
A rich treatment of prayer not as spontaneous spiritual feeling but as a practiced discipline that requires intentionality, structure, and perseverance. Shows how the classical patterns of prayer — adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication — form the soul over time, and why the Christian who prays only when she feels like it will rarely feel like praying.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The evangelical conversation on spiritual disciplines has matured considerably in recent decades, moving past both legalistic and antinomian distortions toward a genuinely gospel-centered account of formative practice. The disciplines are not the Christian life; they are the training ground for it. They are the habits by which the soul is shaped to respond rightly — with trust, with gratitude, with love — to the God who has already acted decisively in Christ. Prayer forms the soul to speak to God honestly and hear from him regularly. Fasting trains the body to say that God matters more than comfort. Scripture reading submits the mind to the authority and wisdom of the word. Sabbath declares, one day in seven, that the world does not depend on our labor. Each discipline attacks a specific form of the self-reliance that is the default mode of fallen human beings.

The practical question for every Christian is not whether to practice disciplines but how to sustain them. Disciplines that begin as rules tend to die when life becomes difficult; disciplines that are rooted in love for Christ tend to grow. The key is to understand each discipline as a form of attention — a deliberate turning of the whole self toward God in the midst of a world that constantly pulls attention elsewhere. Churches that teach the disciplines well help their members see them not as spiritual chores but as gifts — structured opportunities, given to us by centuries of Christian wisdom, to position ourselves before the God who meets those who seek him. That is the logic of the disciplines: they do not earn his favor, but those who practice them consistently find themselves increasingly formed by it.
