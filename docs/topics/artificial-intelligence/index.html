Christianity and Artificial Intelligence

Is AI a tool, a threat, or a test of what it means to be human? How evangelicals are answering, what churches have said, and what pastors and churchgoers think.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is artificial intelligence, and why does it matter to Christians?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is software that performs tasks associated with human thinking, such as writing, translating, summarizing and recognizing patterns, by learning from very large amounts of data. Chatbots such as ChatGPT run on large language models, which predict likely text.

For Christians the first question is less what AI can do than what it is. The Evangelical Statement of Principles (2019) puts the common evangelical answer plainly: technology "is not a moral agent. Humans alone bear the responsibility for moral decision making." See where evangelicals disagree, what the surveys show and short answers to common questions.

Also known as: AI, machine learning, generative AI, chatbots, large language models.

TL;DR

Evangelicals agree on more than the noise suggests. AI is not a person, does not bear God's image and cannot carry moral responsibility; the ERLC said so in 2019 and the NAE said it again in 2026. They disagree on use. Some want AI kept out of preaching, writing and friendship. Many treat it as a tool to use with limits. A growing group of builders says Christians must shape it or cede it. Surveys find pastors more worried than their people that AI will misread Scripture or stand in for God.

In 2019 the Southern Baptist Convention's ethics commission published an evangelical statement of principles on artificial intelligence, before chatbots were part of ordinary life. Now AI drafts sermons, answers Bible questions and keeps lonely people company, and the questions have moved from the seminar to the church office. Christianity Today and The Gospel Coalition have barred AI from the writing they publish. Southern Baptist leaders convened by Lifeway have issued the Brentwood Statement on AI and Christian ministry. The Presbyterian Church in America has a study committee at work, and the National Association of Evangelicals adopted a resolution in October 2026.

What has not moved is the center of the evangelical answer. The major evangelical statements agree that a machine is not a person and that people, not tools, answer to God. The argument is over what follows. Is AI a tool that can serve the church with sensible limits, a formative force that bends its users whatever their intentions, or a frontier the church must help build? The answer shapes what a pastor does on Saturday night, what parents allow at home and what Christians ask of lawmakers.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses

  • Can a machine bear the image of God, think, or become a person?
  • Is it wrong for a pastor to use AI to research or write a sermon?
  • Do AI companions meet a real need, or counterfeit friendship?
  • What should a church's AI policy say, and who should write it?
  • Should Christians help build AI, or keep their distance from it?

The Evangelical Debate

Four Evangelical Approaches to Artificial Intelligence

Evangelicals largely agree that AI is not a person. They divide over how far to let it into the work of the church and the home. Four positions have emerged, and many writers hold more than one. Names are placed only where the person's own words support it; voices from outside evangelicalism are labeled.
Position 1
Resist and Limit
Russell Moore · Collin Hansen · Nadya Williams · Paul Kingsnorth (Eastern Orthodox) · Jonathan Haidt (secular social psychologist)
“We’re not anti-tech. We’re pro-human. We refuse to surrender our minds to the machines.”Collin Hansen, The Gospel Coalition
AI is formative, not neutral. It trains habits, attention and desire, and the companionship, prose and answers it offers counterfeit goods that only people can give. Holders of this view draw hard lines: no AI written content, no AI friends for children, churches kept as places of embodied presence. Most frame it as pro human rather than anti technology.
Key Reads
Chatbot Companionship Will Make Our Loneliness Crisis Worse (Christianity Today) American Evangelicals Long for the Embrace of the Machine God (Mere Orthodoxy) AI Writing: The Day of Reckoning (The Gospel Coalition)
Position 2
Use With Guardrails
Tim Challies · Aaron Menikoff · Mitchell G. Bahnsen · Southern Baptist Convention (2023 resolution)
“For Christians, AI must remain a servant and never become a master.”Tim Challies, challies.com
The broad evangelical middle. AI belongs to the work God gave people to do in the world and can lift drudgery, but it errs, flatters and shapes its users. The counsel runs to verification, disclosure and keeping AI on research and administration while prayer, study and care stay human. The Southern Baptist resolution of 2023 states the posture: neither uncritical embrace nor fearful rejection.
Key Reads
Wise and Helpful Ways for Christians To Experiment With AI (challies.com) Using AI Wisely: A Pastor’s Encouragement to His Church (9Marks) On Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies (Southern Baptist Convention)
Position 3
Shape It for the Mission
Pat Gelsinger (Gloo) · Carey Nieuwhof · Kenny Jahng
“AI isn't just answering questions. It's shaping worldviews.”Pat Gelsinger, Fox News
AI is already shaping what people believe, so Christians must help shape it: testing how the models answer questions of faith, building Christian tools, and putting AI to work in ministry and Bible translation, where it already drafts text for human translators. Most still set limits, but the default posture is adoption and influence. Several of these voices have a professional stake in AI tools, which readers should weigh.
Key Reads
The faith deficit in artificial intelligence should alarm every American (Fox News) Ultimate Guide to AI (For Pastors and the Church) (careynieuwhof.com) AI Doesn’t Have to Diminish Our Humanity (Praxis)
Position 4
Human Dignity First
Jason Thacker · Samuel D. James · Chris Watkin · Pope Leo XIV (Catholic)
“AI can help us in many ways, and used wisely it could bring great benefits. But it cannot kneel.”Chris Watkin, The Gospel Coalition
The image of God is the hinge. Human worth rests on who we are, not on capacities a machine can imitate, so AI is not and cannot become a person, and treating people as machines is the deeper danger. This view carries the argument into transhumanism, medicine, labor and public policy. It overlaps with the first two positions; the difference is that it begins with what a human being is rather than with how AI is used.
Key Reads
You Are Not a Machine (National Association of Evangelicals) An Image of God for an Era of AI (Christianity Today) What Is a Human in the AI Age? (The Gospel Coalition)

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The positions share more than their labels suggest. The ERLC in 2019 and the NAE in 2026 both deny that AI bears God's image or carries moral responsibility, and every position on this page holds people answerable for what they do with it. The real disagreement is about formation: whether a tool that writes, answers and listens can be held at arm's length, or whether using it at all trains the habits it promises to spare us.

That is why the sharpest lines have fallen where formation is most at stake. Publishers have drawn them around writing, pastors around the sermon, parents around companionship. Where the work is clerical, most evangelical writers are relaxed. Where the work is love, study, prayer or care, the weight of opinion runs toward keeping it human. The surveys fit that pattern: Barna found pastors twice as likely to use AI for brainstorming (50%) as for writing or editing sermons (24%), and Lifeway found only 44% of churchgoers see nothing wrong with their pastor using it in sermon preparation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Christianity and AI, Briefly Answered

What does the Bible say about artificial intelligence?

The Bible does not mention AI, but it speaks to the questions AI raises. Genesis 1:26 to 28 says humans are made in the image of God and gives them work to do in the world, which is why evangelical statements locate human worth in who we are rather than in what we can do. Genesis 2:18 says it is not good for man to be alone, a text writers now apply to chatbot friendship. Scripture's warnings against trusting the work of our own hands (Psalm 115, Isaiah 44) speak to the temptation to look to a machine for what only God gives.

Is it a sin to use ChatGPT or other AI?

No major evangelical body teaches that using AI is sinful in itself. The 2023 Southern Baptist resolution calls for "eschatological hope rather than uncritical embrace or fearful rejection," and the pastor Aaron Menikoff tells his church, "I’m not exhorting you to avoid this technology; I’m simply encouraging you to use it well." Sin can attach to the use: passing off AI work as your own, deceiving others, letting it replace study, prayer or real relationships, or trusting it in place of God.

Can AI be conscious, a person, or made in the image of God?

Evangelical statements say no. The ERLC's 2019 statement denies that technology should "be assigned a level of human identity, worth, dignity, or moral agency." The NAE's 2026 resolution says AI "does not bear the image of God; it possesses no conscience, no love, no soul." The Vatican's Antiqua et Nova (2025, Catholic) says AI "should not be seen as an artificial form of human intelligence but as a product of it." Crossway's Samuel James puts it bluntly: "AI does not think. AI does not reason. AI does not contemplate. It is a machine."

Should pastors use AI to write sermons?

Most evangelical voices say AI may help with research or administration but should not write the sermon. Lifeway's Brentwood Statement (2026) says what ministers say and write under their name "must be of our own work." Mike Bullmore asks every pastor, "What am I forfeiting by using AI to do this?" Barna reports that 24% of pastors now use AI to write or edit sermons, and Lifeway found that 44% of churchgoers see nothing wrong with their pastor using AI in sermon preparation.

Are AI companions and chatbot friends wrong for Christians?

The Christian writers cited here agree that an AI companion cannot be a friend. Russell Moore writes that "in disconnecting from real community and cleaving to our own prompts and algorithmically discerned preferences, we are all alone." A Mere Orthodoxy essay calls AI companions a form of pornography, and the director of Harvard's Human Flourishing Program and a tech company cofounder argue in First Things that AI products whose principal goal is artificial friendship or romance should be banned, for adults as well as children. The pastoral counsel is to take the loneliness seriously and meet it with embodied friendship and the church.

What have churches and denominations said about AI?

Key texts: the ERLC's Evangelical Statement of Principles (2019); the Southern Baptist resolution on AI (2023); the Vatican's Antiqua et Nova (2025) and Pope Leo XIV's encyclical Magnifica Humanitas (2026), both Catholic; Lifeway's Brentwood Statement on AI and Christian Ministry (2026); and the NAE's resolution (October 2026). The PCA has a study committee due to report by 2028. Christianity Today and The Gospel Coalition have adopted policies keeping AI out of their writing. See the timeline of key documents.

Can AI help translate the Bible?

Yes, and it already does. Translation teams use AI to produce first drafts from portions of Scripture already translated, which mother-tongue translators then check and refine, and this may shorten the work in the languages that still lack Scripture. The agencies insist that human translators stay responsible for the text; as a member of SIL Global told a 2025 summit panel, reported by Wycliffe, "AI is here to assist, not replace."

Is AI the mark of the beast or the Antichrist?

None of the evangelical statements on this page identifies AI with the Antichrist or the mark of the beast; they treat it as a technology to be governed by love of God and neighbor. The older concern in Scripture is idolatry: trusting something human hands have made in place of God.

What do pastors and churchgoers think about AI?

Lifeway found that 10% of Protestant pastors are regular AI users and 32% are experimenting, and that 61% of churchgoers are concerned about AI's influence on Christianity. Barna found that 94% of pastors and 83% of practicing Christians are concerned that AI will misinterpret Scripture. Pew Research Center found that 73% of U.S. adults say AI should play no role at all in advising people about their faith in God. See the charts.

Glossary

AI terms, in plain words
Artificial intelligence (AI)
Software that performs tasks associated with human thinking by learning patterns from data.
Generative AI
AI that produces new text, images, audio or video in response to a prompt.
Large language model (LLM)
The kind of model behind chatbots such as ChatGPT and Claude. It predicts likely next words from patterns in vast amounts of text.
Narrow AI and general AI (AGI)
Narrow AI does one kind of task; general AI would match human ability across tasks. AGI does not yet exist, and whether it can is disputed.
Hallucination
A confident but false answer produced by an AI model, such as an invented quotation or Bible reference.
AI companion
A chatbot designed to act as a friend, partner or confidant over time.
Digital twin
An AI trained on one person's words, such as a pastor's sermons, that answers in that person's voice.
Agentic AI
AI that takes actions on a user's behalf, such as sending email or booking travel, rather than only answering.
Imago Dei
Latin for the image of God: the teaching from Genesis 1:26 to 27 that human beings are made in God's image.
Transhumanism
The movement that seeks to transcend human limits, including death, through technology.
Deepfake
AI generated audio, image or video made to look like a real person saying or doing something they did not.

The Evangelical Conversation, Curated

Forty pieces from across the evangelical web, grouped by question. Statements and data come first; each group mixes the positions above.

Start here: statements, primers and data

1
Artificial Intelligence: An Evangelical Statement of Principles
Ethics & Religious Liberty Commission
Twelve articles affirm that humans alone bear God's image, deny that AI use is morally neutral, and hold that people may not hand moral accountability to machines. They address medicine, work, privacy, sexuality, warfare and public policy with cautious hope.
2
The FAQs: What Christians Should Know About Artificial Intelligence
Joe Carter · The Gospel Coalition
Written when more than 60 evangelical leaders signed the ERLC’s “Artificial Intelligence: An Evangelical Statement of Principles,” this explainer defines AI, distinguishes narrow from general (“strong”) AI, and doubts that such machines would possess sentience. It is a clear primer on why the church needs to think about AI in light of the image of God.
3
How Should Christians Think About AI?
Samuel D. James · Crossway
James sets aside the yes or no question and evaluates generative AI by biblical principles of creativity, work and relationship. He urges readers to protect their own effort and not let AI substitute for learning, labor or human connection.
4
Artificial Intelligence
Thomas Brewer · Tabletalk
Tabletalk’s editor starts with his own first try at ChatGPT and then lists the risks he sees, including lost privacy, poisoned training data, and addictive artificial companionship. He argues that Christians are called to be lights in the world, not to retreat from it. He also names careful kingdom uses for AI, such as supervised Bible translation, theological training in the Majority World, and lightening the administrative load on churches.
5
Pastors, Churchgoers See AI as Concerning and Confusing
Aaron Earls · Lifeway Research
Lifeway's surveys find 10% of Protestant pastors use AI regularly and 32% are experimenting, while 44% of churchgoers see nothing wrong with their pastor using AI in sermon preparation. Sixty-one percent of churchgoers are concerned about AI's influence on Christianity.

The image of God and what a human is

6
An Image of God for an Era of AI
Russell Moore · Christianity Today
Moore argues the church is unready for AI because Christians often describe the image of God in terms as machinelike as the world's, such as rationality. Scripture presents the image as a who, Jesus the image of the invisible God, rather than a capacity.
7
What Is a Human in the AI Age?
Chris Watkin · The Gospel Coalition
Watkin says Christians should frame AI through the whole biblical story rather than retreat to whatever humans can still do better. Human work flows from relationship with God, human choices carry irreversible stakes, and human relationships are mutual in a way AI exchanges are not.
8
You Are Not a Machine
Jason Thacker · National Association of Evangelicals
Thacker argues that the language blurring humans and machines should be resisted, because human worth rests on being made in God's image rather than on what we can do. He applies that to how Christians describe and use AI.
9
Redemptive Artificial Intelligence
Andy Crouch · National Association of Evangelicals
In a Q&A, Crouch says AI can never bear God's image, which belongs to embodied beings made for love. Technology should be judged by whether it strengthens relational capacity, and AI could even be redemptive if it reduced screen-bound life.
10
Exercising Dominion After Generative Artificial Intelligence
Corey Hastings · Mere Orthodoxy
This essay works from the Pentateuch and names five capacities God gave humans for exercising dominion: making artifacts, creating language, forming relationships, making moral choices, and leading. It then asks how far generative AI functionally imitates each one. Hastings shares the concerns of recent evangelical and Catholic statements, but he argues that AI, “[r]ightly contained,” can help human dominion instead of replacing it.

Preaching and pastoral ministry

11
AI Makes Sermon Prep Effortless. That’s the Problem.
Mike Bullmore · The Gospel Coalition
A veteran preacher argues that AI sermon assistants cut out the heart of preaching. Drawing on 2 Timothy 2:15 and Ezra 7:10, he says that rightly handling the Word requires the preacher’s own labor and study. His test question for every pastor is: “What am I forfeiting by using AI to do this?”
12
AI’s Usefulness and Its Dangers for Preachers
Clayton Chancey · The Gospel Coalition
Chancey explains that large language models predict likely words rather than think, so they can help with research and brainstorming but cannot share in Spirit-dependent preaching. He warns that their fluency fits Paul's warnings against “word-wrangling” in the Pastoral Epistles.
13
The Pastor’s Public Ministry and Generative AI: Perils and Possibilities
Eric Beach · 9Marks
Beach allows pastors to use generative AI in public ministry within limits. He names five risks: plagiarism, skipping careful study of the text, missing application to a specific congregation, lost chances to disciple members, and inaccurate output.
14
Can Agentic AI Pastor Your Church?
Sebastian Y. Kim · The Gospel Coalition
Kim argues that agentic AI able to prepare personalized sermons threatens preparation, shepherding, meditation and the pastor's confidence. He urges pastors to do their own study before consulting AI, know their people firsthand, pray for unction and guard time alone with Christ.
15
The Malnourished Ministry of Relational AI
Joshua Torrey · Christianity Today
Torrey examines the pastor's digital twin and similar tools that promise tireless spiritual care, and argues they ignore human limits, rest, the sufficiency of Scripture and the Spirit's work. Drawing on E. M. Forster's The Machine Stops, he concludes that responsive software cannot minister because it has no lasting commitment to anyone.
16
Using AI Wisely: A Pastor’s Encouragement to His Church
Aaron Menikoff · 9Marks
Originally a letter to his Atlanta-area congregation, this piece gives church members ten questions to ask before using a large language model. They include whether a use will weaken their knowledge of the Word, their dependence on the Spirit, their God-given creativity, or their honesty about plagiarism. Menikoff does not tell people to avoid the technology. He asks that whatever they use AI for be something they would be glad for others to see.

Companions, loneliness and the young

17
Our Desires Need Discipline, Not the Ease of AI
Jay Stringer · Christianity Today
Stringer argues that AI companions appeal to lonely people because they offer closeness without the risk and effort of real relationships. The church's answer, he says, is to form desire through honest community, shared grief and a theology of the body rather than simply to restrict it.
18
AI Companions as a Form of Pornography
Josiah DeBoer · Mere Orthodoxy
DeBoer argues that AI companions, like pornography, promise constant attention, total control and no risk, and both reflect the user's desires back at him. He proposes demanding friendships across ages and social lines as a more durable answer than condemnation alone.
19
‘AI Companions’ and the Christian Doctrine of Friendship
Noah Senthil · The Gospel Coalition
Senthil argues that AI companions marketed to a lonely culture are digital servants imitating personhood, not friends. Christians should answer with real, formative friendships grounded in fellowship with God in churches and neighborhoods.
20
Time to Ban AI Companions
Jonathan D. Teubner and Tyler J. VanderWeele · First Things
A Harvard human flourishing scholar and a tech founder argue that apps built to stand in for friendship and romance should be banned outright, not merely regulated for minors. They distinguish relational bots from general assistants and compare fake relationships to counterfeit money.
21
It’s Not Good to Be Alone, With Chatbots
Greg Cootsona · Christianity Today
Starting from Genesis 2:18, Cootsona looks at recent research showing that roughly one in five Americans aged 12 to 21 turn to chatbots when they are sad or stressed. He takes seriously why AI appeals to lonely teens, since it is always available and always fast. But he insists that young people need embodied relationships, and that parents and churches need to talk with them about AI and their emotional lives, not only about cheating.
22
What AI Is Teaching My Youth Group Girls
Jensen Holmes · The Gospel Coalition
A small-group leader describes a girl in her eighth- and ninth-grade small group who asks AI what to say before she texts a friend. She argues that chatbots keep affirming that users are “not that bad” and that their reactions are justified. That affirmation, she warns, works against seeing sin for what it is, repenting, and finding real comfort in Christ.

Writing, learning and formation

23
Our Line in the Sand on AI: TGC’s Missional Opportunity
Brett McCracken · The Gospel Coalition
McCracken announces that TGC writers may not use AI for ideating, research beyond a basic fact search, organizing, drafting or editing beyond a spelling check, while noting TGC staff use AI in other targeted ways. He frames human-made writing as a missional distinctive.
24
Christianity Today’s AI Use Policy for Writers
Bonnie Kristian · Christianity Today
CT bars writers from using AI for pitches, structure, argument or word choice and bans AI copyediting, while allowing it as a search aid or for transcription if every detail is checked. It admits detection software is unreliable.
25
AI Writing: The Day of Reckoning
Collin Hansen · The Gospel Coalition
Hansen compares AI writing tools to baseball's steroid era, pressuring students and writers to outsource thinking at a cost future generations will pay. He explains TGC's restrictions as a commitment to human thought and writing.
26
TGC, AI, and the Democratization of Competence
Tim Challies · challies.com
Challies argues AI may do for small writers and ministries what blogs did for ordinary voices, giving everyone an editor. He is open to AI as a thinking partner or pressure tester while rejecting it as a ghostwriter or any verbatim AI text.
27
The Danger of AI Isn’t Misinformation. It’s Mis-Formation.
Jonathan Sams · The Gospel Coalition
Sams argues the main spiritual risk of AI is not factual error but formation, since habitual use reshapes attention, expectations of growth, and where believers seek counsel. He gives pastors five principles for keeping AI from crowding out the ordinary means God uses to make disciples.
28
To Efficiency and Beyond: A Warning for AI, Research, Writing, and the Rest of the World
Jordan Steffaniak · Mere Orthodoxy
Steffaniak rejects both the view that AI is evil and the view that it is a neutral tool, and treats AI as a tool built for efficient intellectual production. He argues that when it stands in for research and writing, it stunts the formation those practices exist to produce. He offers a cautious account of responsible use for students and scholars.
29
AI Is Making Humans Dumber
O. Alan Noble · Christianity Today
Noble points to falling reading and numeracy scores since 2012 and heavy smartphone use as evidence that human intelligence is eroding while machines advance. He argues AI can mimic love or art without understanding them, and that humility and moderation are still in our hands.
30
What’s the Point of Education in an Age of AI?
Carrie McKean · Christianity Today
McKean, a West Texas writer, reports what veteran teachers see: widespread student disengagement after pandemic learning loss and the message that AI will outperform them, which she calls a rational response. She argues education is formation in wisdom and virtue, which AI cannot replace.
31
‘Not a Child-Safe Technology’: Proactive Parenting in the Age of AI
Sarah Eekhoff Zylstra · The Gospel Coalition
Drawing on interviews, Zylstra lays out AI's risks to children's schooling, emotional health and future work. She advises close supervision, a life full of in-person relationships and teaching discernment, since specific AI skills will not last.
32
People of the Chatbot
Jeffrey Bilbro · Christianity Today
Bilbro argues chatbots treat Scripture as a customizable private resource rather than a text the church reads together and is reshaped by over time. Memorization, lectionary reading and communal study form readers in ways quick AI answers cannot.

Work, culture and the church's posture

33
Christians Can Give Two Cheers for AI
Mitchell G. Bahnsen · The Gospel Coalition
Bahnsen asks Christians neither to panic about AI nor to idolize it, and to see it as part of humanity's creative stewardship under God. Its dangers come from disordered desire, and with Christian moral limits it can serve human good.
34
AI Can Assist Your Work. But Should It?
Rachel Gilson · The Gospel Coalition
Gilson uses Oliver O'Donovan's account of work's material, social and personal dimensions to judge where AI serves or distorts workers. She asks Christians to separate good challenge from pointless toil, using AI on the second and protecting the first.
35
American Evangelicals Long for the Embrace of the Machine God
Christopher Jones · Mere Orthodoxy
Jones observes that evangelicals have embraced AI more eagerly than most groups, while its most prominent Christian critics are Catholic, Orthodox or mainline. He blames free-market inevitability, theological habits such as pietism and a weak natural law tradition, and a longing for certainty.
36
We’re Asking the Wrong Tech Question
Mitchell East · Christianity Today
East argues the church should first ask whether a technology is worth adopting at all, not how to point it toward good ends. Some technologies are dangerous in themselves, so the church must sometimes refuse rather than give cautious approval.
37
AI Offers Information. God Offers Wisdom.
Vineet Rajan · Christianity Today
A former Marine Corps intelligence officer turned CEO uses the data-information-knowledge-wisdom (DIKW) model to put AI in its place. Machines are very good at collecting and organizing data into information. The relational, high-stakes decisions of life, he argues, require a wisdom that only comes from God.
38
Living for Truth in the Age of AI
Jason Morehead · Christ and Pop Culture
Morehead looks past copyright and energy debates and argues that AI’s worst legacy may be the damage it does to our shared ability to know what is real. He points to AI photo-editing tools that make faked images trivial to produce. Against that backdrop he presses Christians to commit to truth-telling and discernment.

Bible translation

39
How AI Assists in Global Bible Translation
Don Barger · The Gospel Coalition
Barger explains how AI can shorten timelines and lower costs for the roughly 3,700 languages still without Scripture. AI drafts and checks quality, but translators' judgment remains essential.
40
How Should Christians Approach AI Translation?
Phil Thompson · Mere Orthodoxy
Thompson builds a theological case for AI-assisted Bible and other translation. Because AI rearranges signs without an inner word, it can assist while human judgment stays responsible for fidelity to the author's intent.
