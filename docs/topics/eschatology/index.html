On Eschatology & the End Times

Will Christ return before, during, or after a thousand-year reign, and does your eschatology change how you live now?

Updated April 2026

Last updated: November 20, 2026

TL;DR

Evangelicals differ on whether Christ returns before (premillennialism), after (postmillennialism), or if the millennium is now (amillennialism). Premillennialists debate rapture timing. Most agree eschatology should inspire holy living, faithful witness, and eager hope rather than mere speculation, shaping how believers engage culture and anticipate Christ's return.

For much of the 20th century, evangelical eschatology was dominated by dispensationalism, a framework that taught a rapture of the church before a seven-year tribulation, then a thousand-year reign of Christ on earth after his return. John Darby, Cyrus Scofield, and later Tim LaHaye and the Left Behind series made these beliefs central to popular evangelical identity. Entire theologies of history, politics, and mission flowed from this eschatological map. But in recent decades, many evangelical scholars and pastors have begun to question whether dispensationalism accurately reflects Scripture, whether it encourages responsible engagement with culture and suffering, and whether it can sustain genuine hope in a world of real pain.

Today's eschatological debate is not primarily academic. It shapes how pastors counsel their congregation about suffering, how believers approach their calling in the world, and whether the future is something to flee or something to steward. The tension between premillennialism (Christ returns before the millennium), amillennialism (the millennium is Christ's current reign), and postmillennialism (Christ returns after gospel progress ushers in his reign) matters pastorally because each view implies a different theology of hope. If Christ is returning imminently, how do we think about our children's future, about justice, about building institutions? If Christ is reigning now, how do we understand the defeat of evil and the continuity of history? These are not speculative puzzles. They are questions that determine whether we live as those who expect Christ to evacuate us from history or to transform it.

Key Questions

  • Is the rapture taught in Scripture, and does it occur before, during, or after the tribulation?
  • Is the thousand years of Revelation 20 a future reign after Christ returns, or the present age between his comings?
  • What role does national Israel play in biblical prophecy, or has the church become the true Israel?
  • Is eschatology primarily about speculative prediction, or does it fundamentally shape how we live and hope now?
  • How does our eschatology affect our mission: do we evangelize and serve in the world as though Christ is returning tomorrow, or as though we're building for generations?

Four Positions on Christ's Return and the Millennium

The Eschatological Divide

Evangelicals agree that Christ will return in person, judge the world and make all things new. They divide over the thousand years of Revelation 20: whether Christ returns before or after them, and what they are. Here are the four major positions, in the same order as the timelines below. Each quote comes from a holder of the view.

Position 1
Dispensational Premillennialism
Dallas Theological Seminary · John Nelson Darby · C.I. Scofield · John Walvoord · Charles Ryrie
“The next great event in the fulfillment of prophecy will be the coming of the Lord in the air.”Dallas Theological Seminary, Doctrinal Statement, Article XVIII
Christ first comes for his church in the rapture. A seven-year tribulation follows on earth while the church is in heaven. Christ then returns to earth and reigns for 1,000 years, with national Israel restored to her land and God's covenant promises to her fulfilled. The final judgment comes at the close of the millennium. The view holds that God's program for Israel is distinct from his program for the church.
Its holders point to J. N. Darby, the Scofield Reference Bible and Dallas Theological Seminary, and see roots in the church fathers.
Key texts: 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18; Daniel 9:27; Revelation 20:1-3; Romans 11:25-27
Key Reads
Doctrinal Statement, Articles XVIII to XXI (Dallas Theological Seminary) Why Every Calvinist Should Be a Premillennialist, Part 1 (John MacArthur, Grace to You)

Position 2
Historic Premillennialism
John Piper · George Eldon Ladd · Irenaeus · James Montgomery Boice
“My own view is the one represented by Jim Hamilton … historic premillennialism.”John Piper, Desiring God
The church goes through the great tribulation. Christ returns after it, raises the righteous, binds Satan and reigns on earth for 1,000 years. At the end of the millennium the rest of the dead are raised and judged, and the eternal state begins. The view expects no separate rapture and does not divide Israel and the church into two programs.
Its holders point to Irenaeus and Justin Martyr in the second century.
Key texts: Revelation 20:4-6; 1 Corinthians 15:23-26
Key Reads
An Evening of Eschatology (Desiring God) Classic Premillennial View: Why I Take a Premillennial Position (Alex V. Wilson, Christian Standard)
Watch on YouTube
Position 3
Amillennialism
Sam Storms · Augustine · Louis Berkhof · Anthony Hoekema · Kim Riddlebarger
“The NT portrayals of the second coming of Christ forced me to conclude that a millennial age, subsequent to Christ’s return, of the sort proposed by premillennialism was impossible.”Sam Storms, Why I Am An Amillennialist
The thousand years are the present age, between Christ's two comings, with Christ reigning now with his saints while the church on earth suffers and bears witness. Christ returns once, at the end of history, and the resurrection, the final judgment and the new heavens and new earth follow together. The view sees Christ's kingdom as present now and revealed in full at his return.
Its holders point to Augustine, and see it in long-standing Reformed, Lutheran and Catholic teaching.
Key texts: Revelation 20:1-6; John 5:28-29; Matthew 12:29
Key Reads
Why I Am An Amillennialist (Sam Storms) 7 Reasons Tom Schreiner (Tentatively) Holds to Amillennialism (The Gospel Coalition)
Position 4
Postmillennialism
Keith Mathison · Jonathan Edwards · Charles Hodge · B.B. Warfield · Kenneth Gentry
“In general, postmillennialism teaches that in the present age, the Holy Spirit will draw unprecedented multitudes to Christ through the faithful preaching of the gospel.”Keith Mathison, Ligonier Ministries
Christ returns after the millennium, an age of gospel advance in which the Spirit brings great numbers to faith, ethnic Israelites among them. Many holders today take the millennium to be the whole period between Christ's first and second comings. At the end of the age Christ returns, and the general resurrection and the final judgment follow.
Its holders point to the Puritans and to Reformed theologians of the 18th and 19th centuries.
Key texts: Matthew 13:31-33; Habakkuk 2:14; Romans 11:25-27
Key Reads
The Millennial Maze (Keith Mathison, Ligonier Ministries) The Millennium, Part II (Ligonier Ministries)

Where the Texts Divide

The same verses, four readings

The four views often turn to the same passages and read them differently. Each passage below gives the text and then how a holder of each view reads it, in the same order as the positions above.

Revelation 20:1-6

Then I saw thrones, and seated on them were those to whom the authority to judge was committed. Also I saw the souls of those who had been beheaded for the testimony of Jesus and for the word of God, and those who had not worshiped the beast or its image and had not received its mark on their foreheads or their hands. They came to life and reigned with Christ for a thousand years. The rest of the dead did not come to life until the thousand years were ended. This is the first resurrection.Revelation 20:4-5 (ESV)

Four questions decide how the passage is read: what the binding of Satan is, what “came to life” means, what the thousand years are, and what “the first resurrection” is.

Four readings of Revelation 20:1-6
Dispensational premillennial reading

Walvoord sees the binding as future, at Christ’s return, leaving Satan wholly inactive. He takes “came to life” as bodily resurrection, the thousand years in their “literal sense,” and the first resurrection as “an order of resurrection” of the righteous before the thousand years.

John F. Walvoord, The Revelation of Jesus Christ (Moody, 1966), ch. 20
Historic premillennial reading

Ladd sees Christ returning before the thousand years, which he takes as “a real period of time.” He reads the binding as “a curbing of his power and activity,” and “came to life” the same way in verses 4 and 5: two bodily resurrections.

George Eldon Ladd, A Commentary on the Revelation of John (Eerdmans, 1972), on Revelation 20:1-6
Amillennial reading

Hoekema takes the binding as a present restraint, so Satan “cannot prevent the spread of the gospel.” He sees the thousand years as the age between Christ’s comings, and “came to life,” the first resurrection, as believers’ souls reigning with Christ in heaven.

Anthony A. Hoekema, “Amillennialism,” in Clouse, ed., The Meaning of the Millennium (InterVarsity, 1977), section “The Interpretation of Revelation 20:1-6”
Postmillennial reading

One postmillennial reading (Gentry): he takes the binding as begun in Christ’s ministry, the thousand years as “a long and glorious era” before Christ returns, and the first resurrection as spiritual rebirth. He notes the passage is “not determinative for postmillennialism.”

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., He Shall Have Dominion (Institute for Christian Economics, 1992), 69 to 70, 335, 414, 416

Postmillennialists differ on these verses; B. B. Warfield read them much as amillennialists do. B. B. Warfield, “The Millennium and the Apocalypse,” Princeton Theological Review 2 (1904), 604 to 607

1 Corinthians 15:20-28

Then comes the end, when he delivers the kingdom to God the Father after destroying every rule and every authority and power. For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet.1 Corinthians 15:24-25 (ESV)

The question is whether anything comes between Christ’s coming (15:23) and “the end” (15:24).

Four readings of 1 Corinthians 15:20-28
Dispensational premillennial reading

Ryrie reads 15:24-28 as teaching “that Christ yields up the kingdom at the end of the millennium.” Quoting Chafer, he takes the Son to go on reigning under the Father after that, so a thousand-year reign and an everlasting reign do not conflict.

Charles C. Ryrie, The Basis of the Premillennial Faith (Loizeaux Brothers, 1953), 154
Historic premillennial reading

Ladd reads 15:21-24 as “three stages”: Christ’s resurrection, his people raised at his coming, then “the end.” He sees here the “theological necessity” for a millennium between the coming and the end, when Christ’s hidden rule is “manifested in glory in the world.”

George Eldon Ladd, “The Revelation of Christ’s Glory,” Christianity Today, September 1, 1958
Amillennial reading

Riddlebarger reads “then” in 15:24 as “thereupon,” so the end comes “immediately at the time of Christ’s appearing.” He sees Christ reigning now, from his ascension until his return, with no thousand-year kingdom between Christ’s coming and the end.

Kim Riddlebarger, A Case for Amillennialism, expanded ed. (Baker, 2013), 101 to 102
Postmillennial reading

Gentry sees 15:20-28 as “one of the strongest New Testament passages supportive of postmillennialism.” He takes Christ to be reigning now, subduing his enemies in history before he returns, with “no millennial age on the present earth” after his coming.

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., He Shall Have Dominion (1992), 245 to 248

John 5:28-29

Do not marvel at this, for an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment.John 5:28-29 (ESV)

The question is whether this one “hour” holds one resurrection of all the dead, or two resurrections separated in time.

Four readings of John 5:28-29
Dispensational premillennial reading

Pentecost sees the passage as “teaching the universality of the resurrection program,” not “the time at which the various resurrections will take place.” He takes the “hour” as able to span a long period, and reads Revelation 20 as placing a thousand years between them.

J. Dwight Pentecost, Things to Come (Dunham, 1958), ch. 23, “The Resurrections Associated with the Second Advent”
Historic premillennial reading

Ladd reads these verses, with Daniel 12:2, as a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous. He sees it as “impossible to say with any certainty” that they foresee two resurrections, and takes Revelation 20 as the passage that teaches two.

George Eldon Ladd, The Last Things (Eerdmans, 1978), ch. “The Resurrection and the Rapture”
Amillennial reading

Riddlebarger reads the verses, with Daniel 12:2, as “one resurrection in which two distinct groups simultaneously participate.” He sees no hint that the raising of the righteous and of the unrighteous is “separated by a period of one thousand years.”

Kim Riddlebarger, A Case for Amillennialism, expanded ed. (Baker, 2013), 161
Postmillennial reading

Gentry takes the passage as one general resurrection at the last day, of “all who are in the graves,” saved and lost. Quoting David Brown, he reads the “hour” as marking “the unity of period and action,” not a span of a thousand years.

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., He Shall Have Dominion (1992), 286 to 288

1 Thessalonians 4:13-18

For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first. Then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, and so we will always be with the Lord.1 Thessalonians 4:16-17 (ESV)

This passage divides the views on the rapture, not the millennium. The question is whether believers are caught up before a future tribulation, or at Christ’s one visible return.

Four readings of 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18
Dispensational premillennial reading

Ryrie reads the passage as the rapture of the church before the tribulation. He takes it as “a comforting hope” that would lose its comfort if the church “is to pass through the tribulation,” and sees the dead raised then, before Christ’s later coming.

Charles C. Ryrie, The Basis of the Premillennial Faith (1953), 133
Historic premillennial reading

Ladd reads the rapture here as part of Christ’s one return, which he places after the tribulation. He sees it as “very difficult to find a secret coming” in these verses, and takes “meet” to suggest that Christ “continues His descent to the earth.”

George Eldon Ladd, The Blessed Hope (Eerdmans, 1956), chs. 3 and 4
Amillennial reading

Riddlebarger reads the passage as a rapture at Christ’s return that “marks the final defeat of Satan.” He takes the cry of command, the archangel’s voice and trumpet as public, and sees it as “the long-anticipated redemptive-historical climax,” centered on the resurrection of the dead.

Kim Riddlebarger, A Case for Amillennialism, expanded ed. (2013), 162 to 164
Postmillennial reading

Gentry reads the passage as “the visible, glorious, second advent to conclude history,” when Paul “places all believers in the presence of the Lord,” forever. He takes the cry of command and the trumpet in 4:16 as marking “a very public event.”

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., He Shall Have Dominion (1992), 279 to 280

Romans 11:25-27

Lest you be wise in your own sight, I do not want you to be unaware of this mystery, brothers: a partial hardening has come upon Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in. And in this way all Israel will be saved, as it is written, “The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob”Romans 11:25-26 (ESV)

The question is who “all Israel” is, and when and how it “will be saved.”

Four readings of Romans 11:25-27
Dispensational premillennial reading

Ryrie reads Romans 11 as the outline of “God’s program for Israel.” He takes “Israel” in 11:26 as the nation: since Israel was set aside as a nation, “the restoration of Israel is as a nation,” saved before it enters the millennial kingdom.

Charles C. Ryrie, The Basis of the Premillennial Faith (1953), 65, 71 to 72
Historic premillennial reading

Ladd reads “all Israel” as “the vast majority of living Jews,” saved in the future, perhaps at Christ’s return. He takes them to be saved “in the same way as the church,” by faith in Jesus as Messiah, and does not expect a restored temple.

George Eldon Ladd, The Last Things (Eerdmans, 1978), ch. “What About Israel?”
Amillennial reading

One amillennial reading (Riddlebarger): he takes “all Israel” as ethnic Israel, saved just before the end by the same gospel, when God brings “the vast majority of ethnic Jews to faith in Christ.” He sees the Deliverer’s coming as probably Christ’s first coming.

Kim Riddlebarger, A Case for Amillennialism, expanded ed. (2013), 219 to 220

Amillennialists differ on 11:26; Anthony Hoekema read “all Israel” as the whole believing remnant. As reported by Riddlebarger, A Case for Amillennialism, 219

Postmillennial reading

Gentry reads Romans 11 as promising that “racial Jews in great mass will be saved later in the development of the kingdom in history,” before Christ returns. He takes their conversion as completing the gospel’s reach to the nations, with Jew and Gentile one people.

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., He Shall Have Dominion (1992), 206, 230 to 231

Matthew 13:31-33

He told them another parable. “The kingdom of heaven is like leaven that a woman took and hid in three measures of flour, till it was all leavened.”Matthew 13:33 (ESV)

The question is what the mustard seed’s growth and the leaven’s spread picture in this age.

Four readings of Matthew 13:31-33
Dispensational premillennial reading

One dispensational reading (Ryrie): the parables picture “the mystery form of the kingdom” in this age. He takes the mustard seed as “abnormal growth,” its birds as demonized people within Christendom, and the leaven as possibly “evil doctrine” spreading through the kingdom in this form.

Charles C. Ryrie, The Basis of the Premillennial Faith (1953), 97

Dispensationalists differ on these parables; Mark Bailey reads the leaven as “the internal growth effected by the Holy Spirit” in this age. Mark L. Bailey, “The Parable of the Leavening Process,” Bibliotheca Sacra 156 (January 1999), sections “The Analogy of Leaven” and “The Nature of Kingdom Growth”

Historic premillennial reading

Ladd reads both parables as the kingdom present now “in a tiny, insignificant form.” He takes the leaven to mean the kingdom “will one day prevail so that no rival sovereignty exists,” by apocalyptic judgment at the end rather than “gradual permeation.”

George Eldon Ladd, The Presence of the Future (Eerdmans, 1974), 234 to 238
Amillennial reading

One amillennial reading (Riddlebarger): he reads these parables as the kingdom’s advance in this age, alongside opposition. He sees the mustard seed in “the continuing spread of Christianity to the ends of the earth,” with the kingdom advancing “even while provoking men to wrath against it.”

Kim Riddlebarger, A Case for Amillennialism, expanded ed. (2013), 118, 138, 271
Postmillennial reading

Gentry reads the mustard seed as “the extension of the kingdom in the world” and the leaven as “the kingdom’s intensive progress,” penetrating the whole. He takes both parables to mean the kingdom “will grow to dominance in the world” before Christ returns.

Kenneth L. Gentry Jr., He Shall Have Dominion (1992), 238 to 239

What This Debate Reveals

The eschatological divide exposes deeper questions about how we read the Bible. Dispensationalism expects a distinct future for national Israel, amillennialism sees Christ's reign in the present age, historic premillennialism expects a future reign without a separate program for Israel, and postmillennialism expects gospel advance in this age. Each approach takes Scripture seriously but arrives at different conclusions about whether Revelation 20 describes a future earthly reign or a present one, whether Israel and the church are distinct or continuous, and whether the future kingdom is imminent or gradually unfolding. These are hermeneutical questions before they are prophetic ones, and honest Christians can disagree while remaining committed to biblical authority.

But beyond exegesis lies a pastoral reality: our eschatology shapes our hope. If we believe the end is imminent and this world is passing away, we may disengage from long-term justice, institutional faithfulness, and cultural renewal. If we believe Christ reigns now and will reign forever, we may be tempted to equate gospel progress with cultural progress or political power. If we believe the future is genuinely open but ultimately Christ's, we live in a creative tension (neither escapism nor utopianism, but faithful witness in a world that is falling apart and being renewed). The deepest issue in the eschatological debate is not whether the rapture occurs before or after the tribulation, but whether we believe Christ is truly Lord of history and whether his future reign should transform how we live and love and work right now.
