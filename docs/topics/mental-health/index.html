On Mental Health & the Church

Mental illness is real, and the church is learning — sometimes slowly — how to care for those who suffer. What that care looks like is a question of both theology and practice.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is the evangelical debate over mental health?

Evangelicals broadly agree that people with depression, anxiety and other mental illnesses deserve the church's care. They disagree over what mental illness is and who should treat it. The biblical counseling movement holds that Scripture is a "sufficient rule for the presuppositions, principles, and practices of counseling" and denies "that the findings of secular psychology make any essential contribution" to it, in the words of the Association of Certified Biblical Counselors covenant. Christian integration, represented by the American Association of Christian Counselors, joins "biblical truth, clinical excellence, and compassionate care" and treats people as "physical, social, psychological, and spiritual beings." A third emphasis, closest to medicine, stresses that mental illness is illness and that therapy and medication should carry no stigma.

In church life the question usually comes down to referral, medication and what a congregation offers. See what pastors and churchgoers report and short answers to common questions.

Also known as: faith and mental illness, biblical counseling versus Christian counseling, the church and depression, Christian psychology and integration.

TL;DR

Most evangelicals today view mental illness as both spiritual and medical, requiring integrated care. While some emphasize spiritual roots like sin or demonic influence, many recognize biological factors, trauma, and brain chemistry. The answer matters because it determines whether churches offer only prayer or also encourage professional treatment alongside spiritual support.

One in five Americans will experience a diagnosable mental health condition this year. That means every significant congregation has people in its pews who are struggling — with depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, PTSD, OCD, and more — and most of them are doing so in silence, not certain whether their church is a safe place to be honest about what they are experiencing. The stigma around mental illness in evangelical churches is real, even where it is unintended, and it has caused serious harm to real people who needed care and received judgment instead.

The evangelical conversation on mental health has matured considerably in the last two decades. Where an earlier generation of pastoral literature sometimes reduced mental illness to sin or spiritual weakness, a more careful reading of Scripture and a more honest engagement with psychiatry and psychology has emerged. The questions that remain are not simple: What is the relationship between the soul and the brain? When is medication appropriate? What does the local church owe its mentally ill members? How does the hope of the gospel speak to conditions that feel impervious to prayer? These are among the most important questions the church faces in caring for the whole person.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses

  • Is mental illness a spiritual problem, a medical problem, or both — and does the answer matter for how the church responds?
  • What does the Bible say about depression, despair, and the dark night of the soul?
  • How should pastors think about referring church members to secular therapists or psychiatrists?
  • What does a church culture that welcomes the mentally ill actually look like?
  • How do Christians who are mentally ill speak honestly about their experience without losing sight of the gospel’s hope?

The Evangelical Debate

Three Approaches to Mental Health & the Church

Evangelicals agree that mental illness demands Christian care. They disagree significantly on whether mental suffering is primarily a spiritual, psychological, or medical problem — and what that belief means for how churches should respond.

Position 1

Biblical Counseling — Scripture-Sufficient Care

Jay Adams · David Powlison · Heath Lambert (ACBC) · Jay Adams · Joni Eareckson Tada

Scripture is sufficient for addressing all matters of the heart and soul, including conditions that psychiatry classifies as mental disorders. The biblical counseling movement argues that secular psychology often misses the spiritual root causes of suffering and provides solutions that bypass the Word, prayer, repentance, and the local church — the God-ordained means of healing. This does not mean biblical counselors deny suffering or tell people to "just have faith," but it does insist that truly Christian care is centered on Scripture, not psychological frameworks.

Position 2

Christian Integration — Body, Soul, and Psychology Together

Ed Welch · Mark McMinn · Tim Clinton (AACC) · Diane Langberg · Curt Thompson

The gospel addresses the whole person — body and soul — and God’s common grace has given humanity genuine insight into how the brain, nervous system, and psychology work. The Christian integration approach holds that faithful care for mentally ill people will draw on Scripture as the primary authority while using appropriate psychological and medical tools as servants of care. This means both prayer and medication, both pastoral support and professional counseling, both community and expertise working together.

Position 3

Medical Realism — Mental Illness Is Real Illness

Russell Moore · Kay Warren · Amy Simpson · Matthew Stanford · Andy Crouch

Mental illness is real illness — neurological, biochemical, and psychological — and the church has too often responded to it with shame, dismissal, or unhelpful spiritual advice. Just as no responsible pastor tells a cancer patient to skip chemotherapy and pray harder, churches should encourage mentally ill members to access psychiatric care, medication, and therapy without guilt. This position does not minimize the spiritual dimensions of human suffering, but it insists that the church’s posture toward mental illness must begin with compassion, not suspicion.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The emerging evangelical consensus recognizes the church’s unique role: not to replace medicine or therapy, but to offer what only the body of Christ can offer — presence, community, and the integration of spiritual truth with human suffering. The church is the place where the mentally ill can be known without performance, welcomed without pretense, and loved not for getting better but for being part of the family of God. When a congregation learns to see mental illness not as weakness but as suffering that deserves the same compassionate response as any other form of human pain, it becomes a genuine refuge for those struggling in darkness.

The deepest truth binding all three positions is this: Jesus came to heal the whole person. That healing may come through medication and prayer, through professional care and pastoral presence, through the disciplines of faith and the insights of psychology. The gospel’s hope is not that mental illness will never touch us, but that nothing — not even the darkest depression, the most paralyzing anxiety, or the deepest despair — can separate us from the love of God or from the community of believers who are called to bear one another’s burdens. For those in the grip of mental illness, knowing that the church believes this is true, and that it believes it enough to show up and walk alongside, is often the first step toward healing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Mental Health and the Church, Briefly Answered

Is mental illness a spiritual problem, a medical problem, or both?

Many evangelical writers answer some version of both, but they weigh the parts differently. Ed Welch of CCEF, writing for Ligonier, lists five possible causes of depression (our own hearts, other people, our bodies, Satan, and God's sovereign purposes) and advises helpers to treat depression first as suffering and "remain agnostic about its causes." Biblical counselors stress the spiritual roots; integrationists and Christian clinicians give more weight to the body and the brain. The answer shapes whether a church mainly offers prayer and counsel or also urges professional treatment.

Is it wrong for a Christian to take antidepressants?

Writers on both sides of the counseling debate allow it, with different cautions. Desiring God's piece by physician Kathryn Butler (2019) says God "has gifted us with medical science as a means of common kindness" and that, "when carefully combined with counseling and spiritual disciplines, antidepressants can ease some of us back into daylight," though "we should never rely on medication exclusively." Welch writes that people "are free to take medication" but warns that it "cannot address spiritual matters." In Lifeway Research's 2017 survey, 26% of churchgoers said their church encourages medication for mental illness and 6% said it discourages it; most said their church has no opinion or were not sure. See the chart.

Can prayer and Bible study alone overcome serious mental illness?

Many Christians have believed so. In a 2013 Lifeway Research survey, 35% of Americans, and 48% of evangelical, fundamentalist or born-again Christians, agreed that with "just Bible study and prayer, ALONE," people with serious mental illness like depression, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia could overcome it. Ed Stetzer, then Lifeway Research's president, answered that Christians "forget that the key part of mental illness is the word 'illness.'" No position on this page discourages prayer; they differ on what else belongs beside it.

How often do pastors talk about mental illness?

More than they used to. In Lifeway Research's 2021 survey of Protestant pastors, 43% said they speak about acute or chronic mental illness in sermons or large group messages several times a year or more, up from 33% in 2014 (when the question asked only about acute mental illness). The share who rarely or never do fell from 49% to 37%. The same survey found 26% of pastors say they have personally struggled with some type of mental illness. See the chart.

What can a church do for people with mental illness and their families?

Nearly all pastors think it should do something: 89% in Lifeway Research's 2021 survey said local churches have a responsibility to provide resources and support for individuals with mental illness and their families. The most common step is a list of experts to refer people to (68% of pastors say their church keeps one), followed by a plan for supporting families (40%). Fewer offer training for leaders to identify symptoms (20%) or have a counselor on staff skilled in mental illness (18%). Churchgoers often see less of this than pastors describe; see the comparison.

Do churches know when someone is at risk of suicide?

Often they do not. In Lifeway Research's 2017 study, about a third of churchgoers (32%) said a close acquaintance or family member had died by suicide, and only 4% of those said church leaders were aware of their loved one's struggles. Yet 86% said their church would be a safe, confidential place to disclose a suicide attempt or suicidal thoughts, and 80% of pastors said their church is equipped to assist someone who is threatening suicide.

What does the Bible say about depression and despair?

Scripture gives voice to anguish without treating it as unbelief. Psalm 88 ends in darkness; Psalm 42 asks, "Why are you cast down, O my soul?"; Elijah asked God to take his life (1 Kings 19:4); Job cursed the day of his birth (Job 3); and Jesus said in Gethsemane, "My soul is very sorrowful, even to death" (Matthew 26:38). Writers in all three positions on this page draw on these texts to say that lament is a form of faith, not its absence.

The Evangelical Conversation, Curated

1
Hope for the Depressed
Edward Welch · CCEF
Welch’s careful theological treatment of depression from a biblical counseling perspective. He distinguishes between the physical, psychological, and spiritual dimensions of depression while resisting the temptation to reduce it to any one category. The piece offers genuinely pastoral wisdom about what hope actually looks like in the midst of darkness — not the promise that depression will vanish, but the deeper promise that God sees and cares.
2
Friends Are for the Darkness: How to Care for the Depressed
Stephen Altrogge · Desiring God
A practical guide for congregations on how to care for members experiencing depression. This piece pushes hard against spiritual platitudes and offers specific, biblical ways to accompany someone through darkness — including the critical point that recommending professional help is an act of love, not a failure of faith. Essential reading for anyone who has watched depression up close.
3
Scrambling for the Light: Christian Depression and the Use of Medication
Kathryn Butler · Desiring God
Addresses directly the question many evangelical Christians wrestle with: Is it faithful to use antidepressants? This article refuses false binaries between "faith" and "medication," arguing that Christians can and should use medication as part of their response to depression when appropriate. It models the kind of clear theological reasoning that has helped change evangelical conversation on this crucial practical question.
4
The Anxious Church
J. D. Peabody · Christianity Today
Peabody, a pastor who lives with anxiety and OCD, notes that clergy are often the first responders to psychological distress, yet fewer than 10 percent of people who seek a pastor's counsel are referred to mental health professionals. He then gathers counselors, authors, and fellow sufferers, including Steve Cuss, Aundrea Paxton, and O. Alan Noble, to discuss what good mental health is and why churches grow uneasy around unresolved pain, warning that treating depression as a sign of missing God's blessing promotes "a graceless gospel."
5
Counseling Those Who Are Depressed
Edward Welch · CCEF
A foundational piece on how biblical counselors can listen well to those experiencing depression without minimizing their pain or offering false comfort. The article emphasizes the counselor’s posture of humility and patience, recognizing that depression is complex and that real care means walking alongside someone for the long term, not offering quick spiritual solutions.
6
How to Care for Friends with Anxiety and Depression
Emma Scrivener · The Gospel Coalition
Offers practical, gospel-centered guidance on how church members can meaningfully support friends struggling with mental illness. The article insists that appropriate medical and therapeutic interventions are vital, but the body of Christ has a unique role to play in lovingly accompanying those who suffer. It models the integration approach in action.
7
7 Ways to Implement a Mental Health Ministry Plan in Your Church
Stephen Grcevich · ERLC
Practical guidance for church leadership on how to institutionalize care for those struggling with mental illness. This piece moves beyond individual compassion to systemic change — helping churches develop training for recognizing mental health crises, building partnerships with mental health professionals, and creating cultures in which mental illness can be named and addressed with both competence and grace.
8
5 Marks of a Mental Health-Informed Church
Stephen Grcevich · ERLC
Identifies what a church culture that genuinely welcomes the mentally ill actually looks like. The marks include awareness of mental health conditions, recognition of how they affect church participation, basic knowledge of local mental health resources, trauma-informed pastoral care, and open communication about mental illness from the pulpit. Essential diagnostics for any church asking whether it is truly prepared to care.
9
The Heart of Depression
Edward Welch · Ligonier Ministries
Welch explores the multiple dimensions of depression — physical, psychological, relational, and spiritual — without collapsing them into a single cause. He shows why depression is not simply "a matter of faith" or "just chemistry," but rather a complex condition that demands a similarly integrated response. The article models careful theological thinking about mental illness that honors both Scripture and human experience.
10
Anxiety: Sin, Disorder, or Both?
John Piper · Desiring God
An interview addressing one of the most vexing questions in evangelical mental health conversation: Is anxiety sinful, or is it a disorder, or is it both? The discussion wrestles honestly with false dichotomies and shows how a Christian can acknowledge anxiety as both a potential spiritual problem and a genuine psychological condition — and why that dual understanding matters for how we respond.
