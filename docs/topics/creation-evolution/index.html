Creation, Evolution, and the Evangelical Debate

Young earth, old earth, evolutionary creationism—how evangelicals read Genesis, engage science, and hold together Scripture and the created order.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Few topics generate more tension in evangelical communities than the question of how to read Genesis 1 and 2 and how to integrate the biblical creation account with modern scientific understanding. The controversy is not fundamentally about science—evangelical scientists hold a variety of views about the age of the earth and the mechanisms of creation. Rather, it is about interpretation: what does the biblical text teach — a question inseparable from the convictions explored in our guide on biblical inerrancy, and how do we determine that? Young-earth creationists argue that Genesis describes creation in six literal 24-hour days and that the genealogies in Genesis 5 and 11 can be added up to arrive at an approximate date for creation. Old-earth creationists argue that the days of Genesis could be longer periods of time and that the genealogies contain gaps, allowing for an ancient universe consistent with modern physics. Evolutionary creationists argue that God used evolutionary mechanisms to bring about life, with humanity uniquely crafted in God’s image. These are genuinely different interpretations of Scripture, held by thoughtful and faithful evangelicals.

The real issue in the creation debate is hermeneutical and theological, not scientific. How do we determine what Genesis is actually teaching? Is it a chronologically precise historical account, or is it a theologically rich account that uses the literary conventions of ancient Near Eastern cosmology to communicate theological truth? Did Moses intend to provide a scientifically detailed account of creation, or was he addressing the theological question of whether the God of Israel was the sovereign creator of all things? The existence of multiple evangelical interpretations reflects genuine ambiguity in the biblical text itself. Sincere exegetes disagree about whether the Hebrew word “yom” (day) necessarily means a 24-hour period, whether the genealogies are complete or selective, and whether Genesis 1 and 2 are chronologically sequential or present two perspectives on creation. These disagreements do not indicate that one side is being unbiblical; they indicate that the text itself permits more than one faithful reading.

What unites evangelicals across these interpretive differences is a core conviction: God created all things, humanity is made in the image of God and therefore has unique dignity and moral responsibility, and the cosmos is not self-existent or self-explanatory but depends on God’s creative power and sustaining word. Whether God accomplished this through direct fiat or through providential governance of natural processes, whether creation took six days or six billion years, evangelical theology insists on God’s agency and supremacy. The secondary questions about the mechanism and timeline should not divide us from brothers and sisters who share our fundamental convictions about who God is and what he has done. Yet we should also encourage careful exegetical thinking and honest engagement with the scientific evidence, and willing to make the case for God's creative agency — the task of apologetics, knowing that truth in Scripture and truth in nature cannot ultimately contradict each other, even if our interpretations sometimes do.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

What emerges from evangelical engagement with creation and evolution is a framework for thinking about these questions that respects both biblical authority and scientific investigation. Evangelicals overwhelmingly agree that God is the creator and that the universe is not self-existent. Where they diverge is on the mechanisms God used and the timeline on which he worked. This diversity is healthy and should be encouraged, as long as it is held within boundaries. We affirm that God created, that humans bear God’s image uniquely, and that sin entered the world through human disobedience, bringing death and judgment into creation. How those truths specifically relate to scientific questions about age, mechanism, and ancestry requires humility about what Scripture requires and what it merely permits. Many of the most intractable conflicts in evangelical communities about creation arise when we treat interpretive options as if they were doctrinal necessities.

The mature evangelical posture is to be serious about both Scripture and science without reducing either to the other. We read Genesis carefully, respecting its theological claims and its use of ancient literary conventions. We examine the scientific evidence honestly, recognizing that the age of the earth is not determined by biblical arithmetic but by geological investigation. And we hold our particular interpretive commitments with appropriate tentativeness, knowing that we may be wrong about secondary matters and that brothers and sisters who read these texts differently may have equally valid exegetical reasons for their conclusions. What matters ultimately is not the mechanics of creation but the truth that all creation flows from God’s purpose and power, and that we are accountable to our Creator for how we steward his world.
