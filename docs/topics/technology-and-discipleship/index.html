Best Christian Articles on Technology & Discipleship

Smartphones, social media, and AI have reshaped daily life — what does faithful discipleship look like in a digital world?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Every generation of Christians has had to ask: what do the tools and habits of our age do to us spiritually? The printing press, the novel, the television, the internet — each rewired attention, reshaped communities, and created new spiritual hazards and opportunities that earlier generations couldn't have anticipated. The smartphone era is different in degree if not entirely in kind: these devices are uniquely personal, uniquely persistent, and uniquely designed by engineers whose economic incentive is to maximize the time we spend inside them.

The evangelical conversation on technology has matured significantly in the past decade. The early responses were either uncritical adoption (look at what we can do for ministry!) or reflexive rejection (put your phone away). The better thinkers — drawing on theologians like Jacques Ellul and cultural critics like Neil Postman, as well as Andy Crouch's more recent work — have moved toward a more nuanced account: technology is not neutral, but it can be used wisely; the question is whether we are forming it or it is forming us.

For Christians, the stakes are specifically about formation. The question is not merely "am I wasting time?" but "what kind of person is this habit making me?" Scattered attention, comparison-driven anxiety, parasocial relationships that displace real community, outrage as a primary emotional register — these are not just productivity problems. They are spiritual problems. The articles below are the best evangelical thinking on what faithful, intentional engagement with technology actually looks like.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • How is technology shaping our attention, desires, and capacity for deep thought?
  • What does a "tech-wise" church actually look like — and why does it matter?
  • Is smartphone use a discipleship issue or just a time management problem?
  • How should Christians think about AI in ministry and personal spiritual formation?
  • What practices help Christians use technology rather than being used by it?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
The Case for Tech-Wise Churches
Noah Senthil · The Gospel Coalition
The best recent article on what it means for a local church to cultivate wisdom about technology as a community. Senthil argues that digital technology is designed to be addictive and that churches must establish intentional boundaries around screens — not to moralize, but to protect the embodied, present, face-to-face fellowship that cannot be replicated online. A practical and theologically grounded call to action for church leaders.
2
Smartphone Addiction and Our Spiritual ADD
Desiring God
A bracing look at what fragmented digital attention is doing to our capacity for prayer, Scripture meditation, and sustained worship. As digital media fragments attention into smaller and smaller units, this article argues that we must "operate from a God-saturated worldview" — not by avoiding technology entirely, but by developing the countercultural discipline to prioritize embodied worship and deep formation over digital convenience.
3
7 Ways Smartphones Can Enhance Your Spiritual Life
The Gospel Coalition
A necessary counterbalance to pure critique — a practical, constructive look at how smartphones can be intentionally used as tools for spiritual growth rather than distraction from it. Scripture apps, sermon podcasts, accountability tools, prayer reminders: these are real goods when used intentionally. The framing is important: the question is always whether we are using the tool purposefully or being driven by it habitually.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The best evangelical thinking on technology converges on a distinction that should shape every Christian's approach: the difference between using a tool and being shaped by a medium. You can use a hammer intentionally and put it down; a smartphone is an environment you inhabit, and environments form you whether or not you intend them to. The call is not to technophobia but to something more demanding: countercultural intentionality about how, when, and why we engage the digital world.

For the local church, this means the conversation about technology belongs in the pulpit and in discipleship relationships — not just in parenting books. Churches that ignore the formative power of digital media are ceding significant territory in the discipleship of their members to engineers in Silicon Valley whose interests are not aligned with spiritual maturity.
