Best Christian Articles on Fasting

Jesus assumed his disciples would fast. Most evangelical Christians rarely do. Here's the biblical case for recovering one of the most neglected spiritual disciplines.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Fasting is one of the most consistently neglected spiritual disciplines in contemporary evangelical Christianity — which makes it one of the most revealing. A discipline that Jesus assumed his disciples would practice ("when you fast," Matthew 6:16-18 — not "if"), that the early church practiced regularly, and that virtually every serious theologian in church history has commended has nearly disappeared from the evangelical spiritual landscape. Its absence tells us something important about what evangelical piety has become: a spirituality of the mind (Scripture reading, sermon listening) that has lost touch with the body and its formation.

The biblical case for fasting is not difficult to make. It appears throughout the Old Testament as an accompaniment to prayer, repentance, and seeking God's direction. Jesus fasted for forty days at the beginning of his ministry, modeled private fasting in the Sermon on the Mount, and promised to return to his disciples the opportunity for fasting after his departure. The early church in Acts fasts before major decisions. Paul's lists of hardships include hunger and fasting alongside beatings and imprisonments — suggesting he practiced both voluntary and involuntary fasting as part of ministry. The theological question is not whether fasting is biblical but how it works: what it does, why God commands it, and what its relationship is to prayer and spiritual transformation.

The articles curated here represent the best evangelical thinking on fasting — from the biblical theology of the discipline to the practical wisdom of how to begin and what to expect. The question of fasting's relationship to prayer is explored in our coverage of prayer, and its connection to the broader life of spiritual disciplines is developed throughout our topics section.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What is the biblical basis for Christian fasting — and is it commanded, expected, or merely permitted?
  • Why did Jesus say "when you fast" rather than "if you fast" — and what does this imply for Christian practice?
  • What does fasting actually accomplish spiritually — and what are the theological explanations for why it works?
  • How should Christians fast — duration, frequency, types of fasting — and what does wisdom require?
  • What is the relationship between fasting and prayer — and can fasting be separated from its spiritual purpose?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
Fasting for the Sake of the Kingdom
· Desiring God
John Piper's comprehensive treatment of fasting — the piece that has done more than any other to recover serious evangelical engagement with the discipline. Drawing on his book A Hunger for God, Piper argues that fasting is fundamentally an expression of longing for Christ that declares by bodily action what prayer says in words: that Jesus is better than food. Essential reading for anyone wanting to understand the theological logic of fasting.
2
What the Bible Says About Fasting
· The Gospel Coalition
A systematic biblical survey of fasting from Old Testament through New Testament — establishing that fasting is not a peripheral ascetic practice but a pattern woven throughout the biblical story. The piece traces fasting's connection to prayer, repentance, mourning, seeking God's direction, and spiritual warfare, and asks what it means that most evangelical churches have lost touch with this pattern entirely.
3
How to Fast: A Practical Guide for Christians
· Crossway
The most practically useful piece in this collection — a clear, wisdom-based guide to how to actually fast. The article addresses the physical dimension (starting small, hydration, types of fasting), the spiritual dimension (what to do with the time and hunger), the communal dimension (fasting with others vs. private fasting), and the common pitfalls that derail fasting into legalism or performance.
4
Fasting and Prayer
· The Gospel Coalition
A theological exploration of why the New Testament consistently links fasting to prayer — and what the linkage reveals about both disciplines. The argument: fasting intensifies prayer by giving the body a way to express what the heart is saying. The physical hunger of fasting becomes a parable of the spiritual hunger that drives us to God. Separately, each discipline has its value; together, they become something more than their sum.
5
Corporate Fasting: Why Fast Together?
· Desiring God
An exploration of the communal dimension of fasting — one of the most neglected aspects of the biblical pattern. In Acts, the church fasts together before major decisions; in Joel, the prophet calls for a corporate fast in response to national crisis. This piece argues that individual fasting is enriched and intensified by corporate fasting, and that the recovery of fasting in evangelical churches requires recovering its communal form.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The case for recovering fasting in evangelical Christianity is not primarily about discipline or self-denial as ends in themselves — it is about what fasting reveals and cultivates in the relationship between the Christian and God. Fasting says, bodily and publicly, that Jesus is more satisfying than food. In a culture organized around consumption, comfort, and appetite satisfaction, this is a countercultural witness of enormous power. And in a church culture that has made Christianity largely cognitive — something you believe and understand — fasting reintroduces the body as a site of spiritual formation.

The theological logic of fasting is simple but profound: physical hunger, chosen and welcomed for spiritual purposes, awakens and intensifies spiritual hunger. The person who fasts regularly discovers that their appetite for God is not merely an intellectual preference but a genuine longing that can be felt, that grows, and that finds its satisfaction only in Christ. This is why John Piper calls fasting a form of prayer — it is the body praying what the heart longs for. A church that never fasts is a church that has privatized and intellectualized its relationship with God in ways that impoverish its spiritual life.

The Evangelical Debate
Is Fasting Required, Expected, or Optional for Christians?

The primary evangelical controversy about fasting is not whether it is beneficial but whether it is obligatory. Is fasting a spiritual discipline that Christians should practice as part of normal Christian life — something Jesus expected and that the New Testament assumes? Or is it an optional practice that some Christians find helpful and others are free to skip? The answer shapes the culture of entire churches.

Position One
Fasting as Expected Spiritual Practice
John Piper · Dallas Willard · Don Whitney · CCEF
Jesus said "when you fast" not "if you fast" — implying that fasting is a normal part of Christian discipleship, not an optional extra for the spiritually advanced. The New Testament church fasted regularly; the absence of fasting in contemporary evangelical Christianity reflects spiritual poverty, not freedom. Christians who never fast are missing a means of grace that deepens prayer, cultivates dependence on God, and reorders disordered appetites. Every serious Christian should have a fasting practice.

Position Two
Fasting as Optional but Commended
D.A. Carson · Reformed Baptist tradition
The New Testament does not command fasting with the same directness that it commands prayer, giving, or the Lord's Supper. "When you fast" is descriptive of what disciples do, not prescriptive of what they must do. While fasting is genuinely commended and spiritually valuable, it is one discipline among many — and Christians who do not practice it are not disobedient. The emphasis should be on freedom in the Spirit rather than creating a new legalism around fasting.
