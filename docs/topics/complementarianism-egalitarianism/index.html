Complementarianism and Egalitarianism

Gender roles in church and home—the evangelical debate over what Scripture teaches about men, women, leadership, and the ordering of relationships.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Few topics in evangelical life generate more heat and passion than the question of gender roles in church and home. Complementarians argue that the Bible teaches distinct gender roles—that male headship in the church and family is rooted in creation order and reaffirmed throughout Scripture. Egalitarians argue that the trajectory of Scripture moves toward the liberation of women from patriarchal structures and that in Christ there is neither male nor female. These are not superficial disagreements about style or preference; they are fundamental interpretive questions about what the Bible teaches regarding the ordering of human relationships. Both complementarians and egalitarians claim to be submitting to Scripture. Both believe in the full dignity, worth, and value of women. Where they diverge—and it is a real divergence—is in how they read the biblical evidence regarding gender, authority, and leadership. This conversation occurs not in abstraction but in local congregations, where the practical implications determine whether women can preach, whether women can serve as elders, and how marriage is structured.

The complementarian position maintains that God created men and women as equal in worth and dignity but with distinct roles. In the church, complementarians typically believe that the office of elder or pastor should be limited to men, based on texts like 1 Timothy 2:11-15 and Titus 1:6. In marriage, complementarians affirm male headship—the husband’s role as leader and the wife’s role as helper—while emphasizing that headship means servant leadership modeled on Christ’s love for the church. Complementarians insist that this vision does not diminish women but honors creational design. The egalitarian position, by contrast, sees the New Testament declarations of freedom in Christ as the hermeneutical key. Egalitarians argue that the restrictive texts regarding women in ministry reflect the cultural limitations of their time, not the transcultural teaching of Scripture. They point to women prophets, judges, and church leaders in Scripture, and to Paul’s statement in Galatians 3:28 that in Christ there is neither male nor female. For egalitarians, gender equality in all spheres—including church leadership—flows from the gospel itself.

What complicates the conversation is that sincere and scholarly evangelicals, reading the same biblical texts, arrive at opposite conclusions. The meaning of key passages remains contested. What does 1 Corinthians 11:3 teach about headship? How should we interpret 1 Timothy 2:11-15 in light of women prophets in 1 Corinthians 11? Does Galatians 3:28 address church authority or only soteriological equality? These are not easy questions, and pretending they are easy does disservice to both sides. What is needed is sustained, charitable engagement in which both complementarians and egalitarians take seriously the exegetical arguments of the other side, acknowledge the strengths of opposing interpretations, and hold their positions with humility about the difficulty of the text. Most importantly, the debate should never obscure the fundamental agreement: that women are made in God’s image, that their gifts are essential to the church’s mission, that they deserve dignity and respect, and that whatever view we hold should be held in a posture of love rather than judgment toward those who read the text differently.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The evangelical debate on gender and leadership reveals fundamental differences in how Christians read the biblical narrative and apply it to contemporary life. Both complementarians and egalitarians appeal to Scripture, and both claim to be defending the dignity and value of women. The real disagreement is hermeneutical: complementarians see the biblical pattern of male leadership as rooted in creation order and repeatedly affirmed throughout Scripture, while egalitarians see the trajectory of Scripture moving toward liberation from patriarchal structures and understand gender equality as integral to the gospel. These are not peripheral differences. They affect how congregations are structured, who teaches, who leads prayer, and how marriage is conceived. Yet it is crucial that disagreement on this question not become a barrier to Christian fellowship or a test of orthodoxy. Sincere evangelicals of good faith and serious scholarship hold both positions, and we ought to extend charity to those who read the biblical evidence differently than we do.

What should unite complementarians and egalitarians is a commitment to women’s dignity, the affirmation of women’s gifts, and the determination to see women flourish in the congregation and world. Whatever our hermeneutical conclusions about gender roles, we should be horrified by misogyny, appalled by the abuse of authority, and determined to create cultures in which women are honored and heard. The most damaging forms of complementarianism are those that have been weaponized to defend male domination or to silence women’s voices. And the most honest forms of egalitarianism will acknowledge the complexities of certain biblical texts rather than dismissing them. The conversation matters because it shapes the actual lives of real women in real congregations. Our task is to engage it seriously, to read Scripture carefully, to listen well to those who disagree with us, and to ensure that whatever positions we hold are held not as weapons but as convictions we try to live out faithfully in love.
