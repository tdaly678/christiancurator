On Men, Women & the Church

Whether Scripture assigns distinct roles to men and women in the church and home is one of the most contested questions in evangelical life — and the stakes go well beyond church polity.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What are complementarianism and egalitarianism?

Complementarianism is the view that men and women are equal before God as persons but given distinct roles, so that headship in marriage and "some governing and teaching roles within the church" belong to men. Its classic statement is the Danvers Statement of the Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood (1987 to 1988). Egalitarianism is the view that Scripture teaches "the full equality of men and women in Creation and in Redemption," opens every ministry to both, and calls husbands and wives to mutual submission, as set out in Christians for Biblical Equality's Men, Women, and Biblical Equality (1989).

In church life the question usually comes down to whether women can be pastors or elders. A third view, sometimes called soft complementarianism or the third way, reserves the pastoral office for men while opening most other teaching and leadership roles to women. See where churches stand and short answers to common questions.

Also known as: biblical manhood and womanhood (complementarian), biblical equality or Christian egalitarianism, women in ministry, the ordination of women, women pastors.

TL;DR

Evangelicals are divided on this question. Complementarians believe Scripture reserves the offices of pastor and elder for qualified men, citing texts like 1 Timothy 2-3 and Titus 1. Egalitarians argue these passages are culturally bound and that the gospel affirms women in all ministry roles, including church leadership.

The complementarianism-egalitarianism debate is about biblical hermeneutics as much as gender: how do you read Paul's instructions to Timothy and the Corinthians? Are they timeless principles or cultural applications? The question has divided evangelical denominations, shaped ordination policies, and moved from the margins to the center of evangelical culture-war debates. Both sides claim to read Scripture carefully; both accuse the other of letting culture drive exegesis.

The Evangelical Debate

Complementarianism, Egalitarianism & the Middle Ground

The complementarianism-egalitarianism divide has fractured evangelical institutions and denominations. Two exegetically serious positions have defined the debate, with a third attempting to occupy the middle.
Position 1
Complementarianism
John Piper · Wayne Grudem · Thomas Schreiner · CBMW · The Gospel Coalition
Men and women are equal in dignity and value before God but have been given distinct, complementary roles. Male headship in the church (teaching/elder role) and home (husband as head) reflects God's creational design, not cultural accommodation. Paul's restrictions on women teaching men in 1 Timothy 2 and 1 Corinthians 14 are grounded in creation order, not first-century culture. Denying this distorts the gospel metaphor of Christ and the church.
Key Reads
Let Us Reason Together About Complementarianism (The Gospel Coalition) CBMW Resources
Position 2
Egalitarianism
N.T. Wright · Scot McKnight · Mimi Haddad · CBE International · William Webb
The New Testament's trajectory moves toward full equality — Galatians 3:28 ("neither male nor female") is the theological center. Paul's instructions were culturally conditioned correctives, not timeless principles. Women prophesied in the early church (Acts 2, 1 Corinthians 11) and led churches (Phoebe, Priscilla, Junia). The redemptive trajectory of Scripture, followed to its conclusion, removes gender-based restrictions on ministry.
Key Reads
CBE International Resources On Why Patriarchialists Are Losing (Mere Orthodoxy)
Position 3
Soft Complementarianism / Third Way
Tim Keller · Kevin DeYoung · Select TGC writers · D.A. Carson · Andreas Köstenberger
Scripture teaches male headship in the home and the elder/teaching role in the church, but this is a narrower restriction than often applied. Women can preach, teach, lead, and serve in most church functions; only the senior/elder pastoral role is restricted. The cultural baggage of some complementarian applications (women can't teach Sunday school to men?) goes well beyond what Scripture requires.

How to Be Complementarian in the Most Egalitarian Part of the World (TGC) Women in Ministry: Sorting It Out (9Marks)

What the Conversation Adds Up To

What's at stake is hermeneutics (how you read Paul determines everything), ecclesiology (who leads the church), and cultural witness (how the church relates to contemporary feminism). Both sides have serious exegetes; neither has achieved knock-down victory. The practical reality: most evangelical churches practice some form of complementarianism while disagreeing sharply about what it requires in practice.

The evangelical debate on gender and leadership reveals fundamental differences in how Christians read the biblical narrative and apply it to contemporary life. Both complementarians and egalitarians appeal to Scripture, and both claim to be defending the dignity and value of women. What unites them should be a commitment to women's dignity, the affirmation of women's gifts, and the determination to see women flourish in the congregation and world. Whatever our hermeneutical conclusions about gender roles, we should be horrified by misogyny, appalled by the abuse of authority, and determined to create cultures in which women are honored and heard. The conversation matters because it shapes the actual lives of real women in real congregations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Men, Women and the Church, Briefly Answered

What is complementarianism?

Complementarianism holds that men and women are "equal before God as persons and distinct in their manhood and womanhood," and that this distinction shapes their roles. In marriage it teaches the husband's "loving, humble headship"; in the church it holds that "some governing and teaching roles within the church are restricted to men," citing texts such as 1 Timothy 2:11 to 15. The words are from the Danvers Statement, drafted in December 1987 and published in final form in November 1988.

What is egalitarianism?

Christian egalitarianism holds that the Bible teaches "the full equality of men and women in Creation and in Redemption," that the Holy Spirit gives gifts "without preference as to gender," and that husbands and wives live in "mutual submission." It reads Galatians 3:28 as central and reads the few texts that appear to restrict women in light of "the broader teaching of Scripture and their total context." The founding statement is Christians for Biblical Equality's Men, Women, and Biblical Equality, first presented in July 1989.

What is soft complementarianism, or the third way?

It is a position between the two: the office of pastor or elder is reserved for men, but women may teach, lead and serve in most other roles. It has no founding statement of its own, but survey data show it is where many churches stand. In Lifeway Research's 2021 survey of Protestant pastors, 44% of evangelical pastors said a woman could be senior pastor at their church, while 84% said a woman could teach a coed adult Bible study. See the chart.

Which denominations allow women pastors?

Among evangelical bodies, the Assemblies of God, the Church of the Nazarene, the Foursquare Church, the Evangelical Covenant Church, ECO, the Wesleyan Church and the Free Methodist Church USA ordain women as pastors. The Southern Baptist Convention, the PCA, the OPC, the Lutheran Church Missouri Synod and Acts 29 restrict the office to men. The Evangelical Free Church of America does not ordain women but leaves elders to each church; the Anglican Church in North America leaves women priests to each diocese; the Christian Reformed Church lets each congregation decide. Each is checked against its own documents in Where churches stand.

Did the Southern Baptist Convention ban women pastors?

Its confession already limits "the office of pastor/elder/overseer" to men (Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Article VI, amended in 2023). The fight since then has been over writing a ban into the SBC constitution, which takes a two-thirds vote at two consecutive annual meetings. The Law amendment got 61.45 percent on its second vote in 2024 and failed; a new amendment got 60.74% in 2025 and failed. In June 2026 the broader Truth and Unity Amendment, which also covers women preaching on Sunday mornings, passed its first vote with 74.6%. It needs a second two-thirds vote at the 2027 meeting. See the vote chart.

Can women be deacons?

It depends on the church. The PCA and the OPC restrict deacons, like elders, to men. The Southern Baptist confession lists pastor and deacon as the two offices but limits only the pastoral office to men. In Lifeway Research's 2021 survey, 56% of evangelical pastors and 79% of mainline pastors said a woman could be a deacon at their church; among Baptist pastors it was 29%.

Which Bible passages are at the center of the debate?

Complementarians lean on Genesis 2, 1 Corinthians 11:2 to 16, Ephesians 5:21 to 33 and especially 1 Timothy 2:11 to 15 with the elder qualifications of 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1. Egalitarians lean on Genesis 1:26 to 28, Galatians 3:28, Pentecost in Acts 2, and the women Paul names as coworkers, such as Phoebe and Junia in Romans 16; they read 1 Corinthians 14:34 to 35 and 1 Timothy 2 as dealing with "specific, local problems," in the words of the Assemblies of God's 2010 position paper.
