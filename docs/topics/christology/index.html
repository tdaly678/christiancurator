Christology

Is Jesus fully God and fully human at the same time — and why does the answer matter for salvation, prayer, and everything else?

Last updated: November 14, 2026

What is Christology?

Christology is the doctrine of the person of Jesus Christ: who he is, and how he can be both God and man. The Nicene Creed confesses him as "true God from true God, begotten, not made; of the same essence as the Father," who "became incarnate by the Holy Spirit and the virgin Mary, and was made human."

The Council of Chalcedon (451) defined how the two belong together. As Ligonier summarizes the Definition of Chalcedon, "in the one person of Christ are perfectly united the divine nature and a human nature," and the union is "without confusion, mixture, separation, or division, each nature retaining its own attributes." This is called the hypostatic union. See what Americans say about Jesus and short answers to common questions.

Also known as: the person of Christ, the hypostatic union, the two natures of Christ, the incarnation.

TL;DR

Yes, evangelical Christians affirm that Jesus is both fully God and fully human simultaneously—the hypostatic union. This matters because only a fully divine Christ can save us from sin, while only a fully human Christ can represent humanity. This belief shapes how Christians pray to Jesus, worship him as God, and understand salvation.

Christology — the doctrine of who Jesus Christ is — stands at the center of Christian faith. The Nicene Creed's declaration that Jesus is "God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God" affirms what Christians have believed for nearly 2,000 years. Yet contemporary evangelicals wrestle with fresh questions about how to hold together Christ's full divinity and complete humanity without confusion or separation.

At stake are fundamental questions: How could Jesus be truly human while remaining truly God? Did Jesus always know he was God? Did he have human emotions, human limitations, genuine temptation? What does it mean that Christ was "like us in every way, except without sin"? These questions are not merely academic—they shape how we pray, how we understand salvation, and what it means that God became human.

The council of Chalcedon in 451 affirmed that Christ is "one person in two natures," but parsing what that means has occupied theologians ever since. Modern evangelical Christology must answer ancient heresies in fresh terms while remaining faithful to Scripture and the historic ecumenical creeds that guard the apostolic faith.

The Evangelical Debate

Three Approaches to Understanding Christ's Person

Evangelical Christology operates within the bounds of the Nicene and Chalcedonian creeds, but theologians emphasize different aspects of how Christ's divinity and humanity relate. Here are three significant evangelical approaches to understanding who Jesus Christ is.

Position 1
Classical Christology: Full Integration of Divine and Human
Athanasius · Thomas Aquinas · John Owen · Wayne Grudem · Sinclair Ferguson
Christ is one person with two complete natures—fully God and fully human—eternally and simultaneously. His humanity is not diminished by his deity, nor is his deity compromised by his humanity. The incarnation does not mean God ceased being God or that the human nature was absorbed into the divine. Rather, in the person of Christ, the infinite and the finite, the eternal and the temporal, meet without confusion.

The Nicene Creed The Chalcedonian Solution
Position 2
Kenotic Christology: Voluntary Self-Limitation
Gottfried Thomasius · Charles Spurgeon · P.T. Forsyth · John Stott · Millard Erickson
Christ's incarnation involved a real limitation or "emptying" (kenosis) of the use of his divine attributes. Jesus voluntarily restricted his divine powers to live a genuinely human life with real temptation and genuine development. This preserves both Christ's true humanity and his true deity—he did not cease being God, but he limited how he exercised his divine prerogatives during his earthly ministry to accomplish our redemption authentically.
Key Reads
Are Christ's Human Limitations Permanent? What Does It Mean That Christ “Emptied Himself” in Phil. 2:6-7?
Position 3
Lutheran Christology: Christ's Human Nature Shares Divine Majesty
Martin Luther · Formula of Concord · confessional Lutheran churches
Every orthodox Christology holds the communicatio idiomatum, the "communication of properties": because Christ is one person, Scripture can say that God shed his blood (Acts 20:28) and that the Lord of glory was crucified. Lutherans go further. The Formula of Concord teaches that, by the personal union, Christ's human nature truly shares the divine majesty, so that "now not only as God, but also as man He knows all things, can do all things, is present with all creatures." This grounds the Lutheran teaching that Christ's body and blood are truly present in the Lord's Supper. Reformed confessions affirm the communication of properties to the one person (Westminster 8.7) but deny that divine properties pass to the human nature, holding that Christ's divinity is "beyond the bounds of the humanity" while remaining personally united to it (Heidelberg 48).
Key Reads
Formula of Concord, Epitome VIII: The Person of Christ Heidelberg Catechism, Q&A 46 to 49 (the Reformed reply)

What the Conversation Adds Up To

Contemporary evangelical Christology operates within consensus boundaries (the deity and humanity of Christ, the unity of his person, the reality of the incarnation) while leaving room for genuine theological debate about how to understand these realities. The kenotic theologians are not denying Christ's divinity; the Lutheran theologians are not denying his true humanity. All evangelical approaches to Christology are trying to preserve both truths in their fullness.

What unites evangelical Christology is the conviction that Jesus Christ is God in human flesh: in him, the eternal became temporal, the infinite became finite, and the sinless One bore the sin of the world. How we understand this mystery shapes our worship, our assurance, our prayer, and our hope. The study of Christology is never merely academic; it is always doxological, leading us deeper into wonder at the person and work of Christ.

Frequently Asked Questions

Christology, Briefly Answered

What is the hypostatic union?

It is the teaching that Christ is one person with two natures, one divine and one human. Ligonier's summary of the Definition of Chalcedon puts it plainly: "Christ is one person with two natures." Because the natures are not mixed, "He is not a third kind of being"; because they are not separated, "the same Christ acts" whether he is exercising divine or human attributes.

Is Jesus a created being?

Historic Christian teaching says no. The Nicene Creed calls the Son "begotten from the Father before all ages" and "begotten, not made," against Arius, who taught that the Son was created. Yet in the 2022 State of Theology survey, 73% of Americans with evangelical beliefs agreed that "Jesus is the first and greatest being created by God." See the chart.

What is kenotic Christology?

It takes its name from Philippians 2:7, where Christ "emptied himself" (Greek kenosis). Kenotic theologians hold that in the incarnation the Son limited the use of his divine attributes. Classical Reformed teachers read the verse differently. Ligonier's answer to What does it mean that Christ "emptied Himself"? says it "does not mean that Christ became anything less than He was. It does not mean that He ceased to be God," but that he submitted himself "to the power of the Holy Spirit and the will of the Father."

Can we say that God died on the cross?

Classical Christology says the attributes of each nature belong to the one person, so we may say what the person did through either nature. Ligonier's summary of Chalcedon gives the example: "Christ's divine nature, for example, does not have blood, but the person of Christ does," citing Acts 20:28, where the church is the one God "obtained with his own blood." This is the communication of attributes (communicatio idiomatum). Lutheran and Reformed theologians have long disagreed over how far it reaches.

Do evangelicals believe Jesus is God?

Most do, but not all who hold evangelical beliefs say so on a survey. In the 2025 State of Theology survey, 68% of Americans with evangelical beliefs disagreed that "Jesus was a great teacher, but he was not God"; among all U.S. adults, 49% agreed (Lifeway Research). See the chart.

The Evangelical Conversation, Curated

1
On Christology
Robbie F. Castleman · The Gospel Coalition
Writing in Themelios, Castleman argues that Christology matters because it is about the historical Jesus: God who took on our flesh, lived and suffered in it, rose and ascended. She shows how our Christology shapes our soteriology, so that weakening Christ’s full divinity also shrinks our sense of sin and of grace. A short, pastoral case for why classical teaching on Christ’s person still matters for faith today.
2
Jesus Is Fully Human
David Mathis · Desiring God
Mathis asks whether believers wrestle more with the deity of Jesus or with his humanity, and argues that many evangelicals who readily confess him as Lord have not reckoned with how fully “the Word became flesh.” He traces how the church has swung between denying his divinity and shrinking his manhood. A pastoral essay that shows why the full humanity of Christ matters for faith and worship, not only for doctrine.
3
Scripture and the Two Natures of Christ
Ligonier Ministries
This Ligonier devotional on John 1:17 restates the historic teaching that Christ is one person with a divine and a human nature, each retaining its own properties. It answers thinkers who argue from Christ's true humanity that he could err, and from there that Scripture may contain errors. Its reply is that lacking knowledge, as when Jesus asked who touched his garment (Luke 8:45-46), is not the same as error, so a full humanity does not require a fallible Christ or a fallible Bible.
4
What Is the Gospel?
9Marks
A short 9Marks primer, partly adapted from Mark Dever's The Gospel and Personal Evangelism, that summarizes the gospel as the good news of what Jesus Christ has done to reconcile sinners to God. It unpacks the message under four headings: God, Man, Christ, and Response. Its Christ section states the classic confession that Jesus is fully God and fully man, lived a sinless life, died bearing God's wrath for all who believe, and rose from the grave.
5
10 Things You Should Know about the Incarnation
Stephen J. Wellum · Crossway
Wellum condenses classical Christology into ten theses: the eternal Son is the subject of the incarnation, the incarnation is an act of addition rather than subtraction, and the human nature he assumed is complete and sinless. He also explains how the Son lived within the limits of his human nature without ceasing to act through his divine nature. A compact, carefully worded summary for readers who want the doctrine stated precisely.
6
Kenosis: The Self-Emptying of Christ in Scripture and Theology
Thomas Haviland-Pabst · Reformed Faith & Practice
Haviland-Pabst reviews the Eerdmans volume edited by Paul T. Nimmo and Keith L. Johnson, an informal festschrift for Bruce McCormack, for the RTS journal Reformed Faith & Practice. He notes the worry that "kenosis" usually names the aberrant claim that the Son gave up his divine attributes, and finds that several essays instead defend an orthodox reading of Christ's humility, while others, such as Grant Macaskill's, depart from traditional Reformed Christology. He concludes that the book must be read with discernment but is a significant contribution to scholarship on the incarnation.
7
The Incarnation Sheds Light on Astrophysics
Deborah Haarsma · Christianity Today
Haarsma, an astrophysicist, recalls hunting gravitational lenses at the Very Large Array and wrestling as a young researcher with the claim that our tiny planet is insignificant. She notes Barna research showing that science ranks among the top reasons people doubt Christianity, and argues the church must bring the discoveries of modern science together with ancient Christian truth. Her answer centers on the incarnation: the Creator of an unfathomably vast cosmos chose to become human on a planet that is a pinprick in space.
8
Recent trends in Christology
Gerald Bray · The Gospel Coalition
Writing in Themelios, Bray surveys a decade of Christological study that began with the 1977 symposium The Myth of God Incarnate, which most scholars quickly criticized. He shows how recent work divides over whether it accepts the Chalcedonian heritage, a split that tracks a preference for ontological or functional approaches to the figure of Jesus. Along the way he assesses replies such as those of I. H. Marshall and C. F. D. Moule and functional proposals such as Schubert Ogden's.
