Best Christian Articles on Politics & the Church

Between withdrawal and capture — what does faithful Christian civic engagement actually look like?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

The question of how Christians relate to political power is not new — it runs from Romans 13 and 1 Peter 2 through Augustine's two cities, Luther's two kingdoms, and Abraham Kuyper's sphere sovereignty, all the way to the present debates about Christian nationalism. What is new is the intensity of the current moment: evangelicals in America find themselves deeply politically polarized, with some voices calling for Christians to seize the levers of government and others calling for radical disengagement from partisan politics entirely.

Neither extreme is faithful to Scripture or history. The biblical vision is more demanding than either: Christians are called to be genuinely engaged with the political order as a matter of neighbor love and justice, while maintaining a prophetic distance from all earthly political programs that would compromise their ultimate allegiance to the Kingdom of God. The church is not a political party, and no political party is the church. The failure to maintain that distinction has cost evangelical witness dearly.

What distinguishes the best evangelical writing on this topic is the insistence that politics must be discipled — that Christians bring their politics under the authority of Scripture, the community of the local church, and the formation that comes from the full counsel of God, rather than importing partisan frameworks and fitting Christianity around them. These articles represent that tradition of engaged, critical, gospel-shaped political thinking.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What does the Bible actually say about Christians' relationship to government and political power?
  • What is Christian nationalism, and how should evangelicals respond to it?
  • How does the local church disciple the political lives of its members?
  • What is the difference between "jagged-line" issues (where Christians may disagree) and clear-line issues?
  • How can Christians maintain political engagement without political captivity?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
Why We Need the Church to Disciple Our Politics
The Gospel Coalition
The most important framing piece in this collection. The argument is simple and devastating: when churches remain silent on how Christians should think politically, all our opinions come from somewhere else — cable news, social media, partisan friends — rather than from Scripture and the guidance of a faithful community. The church's silence on politics is not neutrality; it is an abdication that cedes formation to other, less trustworthy teachers.
2
The Christian Calling to Citizenship
The Gospel Coalition
A positive vision for why Christians engage politically at all — not as a path to power, but as an expression of neighbor love and stewardship of the political sphere God has entrusted to human governance. This article argues that a Christian worldview produces a distinct political apologetic: engagement for the glory of God and the good of our neighbors, not the triumph of a partisan tribe.
3
6 Ways Christians Can Be Wiser in Political Engagement
The Gospel Coalition
A practical piece on the habits and postures that distinguish faithful political engagement from partisan fever. Avoiding tribal epistemology, maintaining friendships across political lines, keeping eschatological perspective — these are not just social courtesies but discipleship practices. The article makes a compelling case that how Christians engage politically is itself a witness to the gospel.
4
Politics, Conscience, and the Church
The Gospel Coalition — Themelios
A more academic treatment that addresses one of the hardest practical questions: how do Christians who care about justice reach different political conclusions, and how does the church hold them together? The distinction between "clear-line" issues (where Scripture speaks directly) and "jagged-line" issues (where faithful Christians may disagree) is foundational for churches trying to navigate political diversity without either imposing partisan uniformity or abandoning prophetic witness.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The evangelical conversation on politics is genuinely fractured right now, and any honest curation has to acknowledge that. There are serious Christian thinkers who believe the church must pursue cultural and political influence more aggressively, and serious Christian thinkers who believe the church must recover its prophetic distance from all partisan programs. What unites the best writers on all sides is a commitment to thinking about politics theologically rather than consuming politics tribally.

The deepest issue may be eschatological: what do we actually believe God is doing in history, and what is the church's role in it? Christians who believe the Kingdom advances through political victory will engage politics differently than Christians who believe the church's primary witness is a distinctive community that embodies the coming Kingdom now. Both views are present within evangelicalism; both deserve engagement rather than caricature.
