What Is Evangelicalism? The Best Articles

The word 'evangelical' is in crisis — claimed by too many, defined by too few, and increasingly identified with things that have little to do with the gospel.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Few words in contemporary Christianity are more contested than "evangelical." It was not always so. Historian David Bebbington's famous quadrilateral — biblicism, crucicentrism, conversionism, and activism — provided a working definition that most serious students of the movement accepted for decades. But the political and cultural upheavals of the past decade have strained that definition nearly to breaking point. Polling data now shows that "evangelical" in American usage correlates more strongly with political identity than with theological conviction. People who rarely attend church and do not hold traditional Christian beliefs claim the label; theologically serious evangelicals are increasingly reluctant to use it.

The crisis of evangelicalism is partly external — the entanglement with partisan politics that has made the word toxic to millions — and partly internal. Progressive evangelicalism has been revising historic evangelical positions on Scripture, sexuality, and salvation in ways that raise fundamental questions about whether the movement's theological commitments are stable. Confessional evangelicalism, represented by institutions like The Gospel Coalition, 9Marks, and Reformed seminaries, insists that these commitments are non-negotiable and that their revision marks a departure from evangelicalism, not its development. The sociological and theological definitions of "evangelical" have come apart, and the question of which one matters more is one of the most important identity debates in American Christianity.

The articles here represent the best evangelical reflection on the movement's identity, its crisis, and its future — from historical accounts of what evangelicalism has been to sharp analysis of what it is becoming and what it should be. Questions about evangelicalism's relationship to American politics connect directly to our coverage of politics and the church, and questions about its theological identity connect to our coverage of biblical inerrancy and the atonement.

  • What is the Bebbington Quadrilateral — and is it still an adequate definition of evangelical Christianity?
  • Is "evangelical" primarily a theological category or a sociological/political one — and does that distinction matter?
  • What is progressive evangelicalism, and how does it differ from confessional evangelicalism?
  • Has the evangelical church in America become captive to political identity — and what would recovery look like?
  • What is the future of the evangelical movement — and should serious Christians continue to use the label?

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The evangelical movement is in genuine crisis — not primarily because of external pressure but because of internal confusion about what it is and what it is for. When the word 'evangelical' becomes a political identification, when polling data shows that many who claim the label hold theological views incompatible with historic evangelical commitments, and when the movement's most visible representatives seem more concerned with cultural power than gospel proclamation, something has gone deeply wrong.

The response to this crisis cannot be merely sociological — a rebranding exercise that jettisons the word 'evangelical' for something less politically loaded. The crisis is theological, and the only adequate response is a theological one: a recovery of the convictions that made evangelicalism a meaningful category in the first place. Biblicism, crucicentrism, conversionism, and activism are not arbitrary traditions; they are the theological DNA of a movement that has historically been characterized by genuine encounter with the gospel. The question is whether that encounter can be recovered from beneath the cultural and political accretions that have nearly buried it.

The Evangelical Debate
Confessional Evangelicalism vs. Progressive Evangelicalism

The sharpest theological controversy within evangelicalism is not between evangelicals and the world but between two versions of evangelicalism itself: a confessional evangelicalism that holds to historic positions on Scripture, sexuality, and salvation, and a progressive evangelicalism that is revising those positions in dialogue with contemporary culture. The stakes are not peripheral — they concern the nature of Scripture's authority and the content of the gospel.

Position One
Confessional Evangelicalism
D.A. Carson · Tim Keller · John Piper · Kevin DeYoung · The Gospel Coalition
The theological commitments of evangelical Christianity — the full authority of Scripture, personal conversion, the penal substitutionary atonement, and traditional sexual ethics — are not cultural traditions to be revised but gospel-defining convictions to be maintained. Progressive revision of these positions does not represent evangelical development but evangelical departure. The crisis of evangelicalism is not that confessional Christians have been too rigid but that the movement has drifted from its theological moorings.

Position Two
Progressive Evangelicalism
Brian McLaren · Rachel Held Evans · Rob Bell · Tony Jones · Emergent tradition
Evangelicalism must engage seriously with cultural and intellectual challenges to its traditional positions rather than simply restating them. Progressive evangelicals argue that historic evangelical positions on gender, sexuality, biblical inerrancy, and hell are cultural constructions that can be revised without losing the gospel's core. The movement must be willing to change in order to be faithful. The confessional wing mistakes cultural forms for gospel essentials.
