Best Christian Articles on Prayer

Why do we pray if God already knows everything — and what does serious prayer actually look like?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Prayer is simultaneously the simplest and most mysterious act in the Christian life. Every believer knows they should pray. Most feel they do it poorly. Fewer have thought carefully about what prayer actually is — and why the God who governs all things would want us to ask him for anything at all. The theology of prayer is not a detour from the practice of prayer; it is the foundation that makes the practice coherent.

The Reformed tradition has contributed distinctive insights to evangelical thinking about prayer. Because God is sovereign, prayer is not an attempt to change his mind or inform him of things he doesn't know. Instead, prayer is the ordained means through which God accomplishes his purposes — the relational, dependent posture through which finite creatures participate in the work of an infinite God. The Westminster Shorter Catechism defines prayer as "an offering up of our desires unto God for things agreeable to his will," and that phrase "agreeable to his will" carries enormous theological freight.

The other dimension often neglected is corporate prayer. Evangelicalism has so thoroughly privatized prayer — devotional prayer, personal quiet time, individual intercession — that many Christians have little experience of what it means for a congregation to pray together as a body. The New Testament, however, presents corporate prayer as central to the life of the gathered church, not a supplementary activity for the especially spiritual. The articles below address both dimensions: the theology that grounds prayer and the practice that gives it form.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • If God is sovereign, why does prayer change anything?
  • What does the Trinity teach us about the nature and structure of prayer?
  • What is the difference between prayer that flows from faith and prayer that is merely formulaic?
  • How should a local church cultivate a culture of corporate prayer?
  • What can we learn from the Puritans — and from the Psalms — about how to pray well?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
A Summary Theology of Prayer
John Piper · Desiring God
The essential doctrinal foundation piece. Piper argues that understanding God's purpose in prayer — specifically, that prayer is designed to glorify God by making his people dependent on him — deepens rather than undermines the motivation to pray. The paradox at the heart of prayer (we ask an omniscient God who already knows our needs) finds its resolution here in a God-centered theology of petition and praise.
2
What the Doctrine of the Trinity Means for Prayer
9Marks
One of the most theologically rich short pieces on prayer available. The Trinitarian shape of Christian prayer — addressed to the Father, through the Son, in the power of the Spirit — is not liturgical decoration but a description of the actual structure of what prayer is. This article shows that Trinitarian theology is not an abstract doctrine for seminarians but the living grammar of every Christian's prayer life.
3
A Biblical Theology of Corporate Prayer
9Marks
A thorough survey of what the New Testament church actually looked like when it prayed together. Drawing on Acts and the apostolic letters, this article makes the case that corporate prayer — the congregation gathered to ask God together — was central to the early church's life in a way that most evangelical churches have not recovered. A convicting and constructive read for church leaders thinking about Sunday morning practices.
4
The Breath Behind Our Prayers: How the Spirit Draws Us to God
Desiring God
An essential corrective to the common assumption that prayer is primarily our initiative. This article explores the role of the Holy Spirit in prayer — how the Spirit intercedes within us, drawing us to the Father through the Son, and how "true prayer depends on the Holy Spirit, by whose power alone we approach the Father." For Christians who feel their prayer life is dry or forced, this reframes prayer as something the Spirit is doing in and through us.
5
Does God Hear Scripted Prayers? Lessons from a Puritan Controversy
Desiring God
A fascinating historical piece that excavates a real Puritan debate — between John Owen and John Bunyan on one side, and Richard Baxter on the other — about whether written or liturgical prayers are appropriate in Christian worship. The debate illuminates a perennial tension between spontaneity and form in prayer, and offers wisdom for contemporary evangelicals navigating discussions about liturgy, prayer books, and free prayer.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The articles in this collection share a conviction that prayer is neither a technique for getting things from God nor a meditation practice for spiritual self-improvement. It is a covenantal relationship — the creature addressing the Creator, the child speaking to the Father — grounded in the work of Christ and empowered by the Spirit. That theological foundation is what distinguishes Christian prayer from every other form of spirituality.

The practical implication is that prayer needs to be recovered both personally and corporately. Evangelicals have rich traditions of personal devotion and intercessory prayer that are worth reclaiming. And the New Testament's vision of the gathered community united in prayer before God — as a central, not peripheral, act of congregational worship — is one the church is only beginning to rediscover.
