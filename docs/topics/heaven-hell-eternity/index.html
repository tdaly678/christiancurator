Heaven, Hell, and Eternal Life

What awaits after death—the evangelical debate over judgment, annihilation, and the nature of resurrection life in the age to come.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Eschatology—the study of last things—has always been a contested domain in Christian theology, but few eschatological doctrines are more fraught in contemporary evangelicalism than the nature of hell. The traditional evangelical view holds that hell is a place of conscious, eternal punishment where those who reject Christ suffer the just consequences of their rebellion. Yet this doctrine is increasingly rejected by progressive evangelicals and questioned even by some conservative believers who find it difficult to reconcile with God’s love. Annihilationism—the view that the unsaved will cease to exist rather than suffer eternally—has gained traction as a seemingly more merciful alternative. Universalism—the belief that all will ultimately be saved—is no longer limited to liberal Protestantism but is creeping into evangelical communities. These developments have forced evangelical scholars and pastors to defend and clarify the doctrine of hell with greater care and precision. What becomes clear in the conversation is that how we think about hell shapes how we think about the gospel, the nature of sin, and the character of God.

Equally important to the evangelical hope is not what we escape but what we move toward. The popular imagination of heaven as disembodied spiritual existence on clouds singing hymns forever bears little resemblance to what Scripture teaches. The biblical hope is not heaven as an eternal destination but rather the new heavens and new earth—a renewed creation where God dwells with his people, where justice is established, where suffering and death are no more. This is not an escape from the physical world but a redemption of it. The resurrection is not merely the resurrection of the soul but of the whole person, body and soul. Christians will not spend eternity in a ghostly realm but in a renewed cosmos. This doctrine is not merely speculative; it has profound implications for how we live now. If this world is destined for renewal rather than destruction, if creation matters, if our bodies matter—then environmental stewardship matters, art and culture matter, justice and mercy matter. They are not peripheral concerns of Christians worried about politics but central concerns of people who believe that God is renewing all things.

The conversation about end times also encompasses the timeline and nature of Christ’s return. Evangelicals hold to a bewildering array of eschatological positions—premillennialism, amillennialism, postmillennialism; pretribulationism, midtribulationism, posttribulationism. The fact that sincere and scholarly evangelicals can read the same biblical texts and arrive at wildly different chronologies suggests that these are genuinely difficult questions. What unites evangelicals across these interpretive differences is the conviction that Christ will return, that he will judge all people, and that he will establish a kingdom of righteousness and peace. The specifics of how and when matter less than the certainty that history is moving toward Christ’s triumph and that believers should live in light of that hope. In a culture of despair — one that intersects with the crisis explored in our guide on mental health and faith —, evangelical eschatology offers a radical alternative: the conviction that God is not finished with the world, that evil will not have the final word, and that all things are being restored toward Christ and his purposes.

What Does the Bible Say About Hell?

The definitive TGC reference essay on hell—surveying Old Testament Sheol, Jesus’s teaching on Gehenna, and Paul’s language of eternal destruction. Addresses annihilationism and universalism directly while defending the traditional evangelical view of conscious, eternal punishment.

Is Hell Real?
· Desiring God
A pastoral and theological defense of hell’s reality in a cultural moment when the doctrine is widely dismissed or sentimentalized. Shows why a God who does not punish evil is not good—and why the reality of hell is necessary for understanding the magnitude of what Christ endured on the cross.

· Desiring God
A biblical-theological answer to the most-asked eschatological question. Corrects the popular image of heaven as disembodied spiritual existence on clouds, presenting instead the biblical hope of resurrection, new creation, and embodied life with God—which is far more glorious than the greeting-card version.

· The Gospel Coalition
A clear and fair treatment of annihilationism—the view that the unsaved will ultimately cease to exist rather than suffer eternally. · Ligonier Ministries
R.C. Sproul on the ultimate Christian hope: not escape from the material world but its renewal. The new creation is the fulfillment of what was begun in Eden—and understanding this transforms how Christians live now, in their bodies and in the world.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The evangelical conversation on heaven and hell reveals that eschatology cannot be cordoned off as a peripheral concern. How we think about the end shapes how we think about everything else. The doctrine of hell, whatever its precise details, testifies to the seriousness of sin and the weight of eternal consequences. It explains why the gospel is urgently necessary and why Jesus’s death and resurrection are not optional improvements to human life but the only way of salvation — which is why the doctrine of the atonement is so closely bound to our understanding of judgment. The reality of judgment—whether one holds to eternal conscious punishment, annihilation, or some other view—establishes that God takes sin seriously enough to judge it, and that decisions made in this life have eternal weight. On the positive side, the doctrine of the new heavens and new earth offers an alternative to both escapism and despair. We are not working toward the destruction of the material world but toward its redemption. Creation matters. Culture matters. Justice matters. Our bodies matter. All of these will be redeemed and renewed in the age to come. This doctrine calls us to live not as pilgrims just passing through but as stewards of a world destined for eternal renewal.

What emerges across evangelical voices on these topics is an eschatology that is simultaneously cosmic in scope and intimate in promise. Christ is returning not to extract believers from the world but to judge all things, establish justice, and restore creation. God is not writing off his creation but redeeming it. Believers await not a ghostly eternity in heaven but embodied life in a renewed cosmos, where God dwells with his people and all tears are wiped away. This hope is not escapism—it is the ground of faithful work and witness in the present. Because we believe the future is secure in God’s hands, we can labor for justice, pursue redemption, cultivate beauty, and serve our neighbors with urgent love, knowing that these labors are not futile but are being gathered up into God’s eternal purposes. In this way, Christian eschatology is not primarily about predicting the future but about living faithfully in the present, anchored in the conviction that God’s kingdom will come and his will be done.
