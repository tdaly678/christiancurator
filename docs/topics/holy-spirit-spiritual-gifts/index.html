On the Holy Spirit & Spiritual Gifts

Do miraculous spiritual gifts like tongues and prophecy continue today, or did they cease with the apostolic age — and how does the answer shape Christian life and worship?

Last updated: July 18, 2026

TL;DR

Evangelicals agree the Holy Spirit indwells and empowers every believer, but disagree over whether miraculous gifts like tongues, prophecy, and healing continue today. Cessationists hold that those gifts ended with the apostolic age; continuationists and Pentecostals believe they remain available until Christ returns. The answer shapes how churches worship, pray, and expect God to act.

Walk into two faithful evangelical churches on a Sunday morning and you may encounter two very different sets of expectations. In one, members pray aloud in tongues, share prophetic impressions, and gather at the front for healing prayer. In the other, the service centers on the preached Word, and the congregation would regard those same practices as belonging to a completed chapter of church history. Behind the difference lies one of the most practically consequential debates in evangelical theology: did the miraculous gifts of the Spirit — prophecy, tongues, healing, miracles — cease with the apostles, or do they continue today?

This is not a debate between Christians who take the Bible seriously and Christians who don't. Cessationists build their case on Scripture's own account of the apostles' unique, foundational role and the finality of God's revelation in Christ. Continuationists build theirs on Paul's explicit commands to "earnestly desire" the gifts and the absence of any text announcing their withdrawal. Meanwhile, the global Pentecostal and charismatic movement — the fastest-growing segment of Christianity over the past century — treats the gifts as ordinary equipment for church life and mission. The stakes are pastoral as much as doctrinal: how does God speak and act today, what should believers expect when they pray for the sick, and what does a Spirit-filled church actually look like?

Key Questions This Topic Addresses

  • Did miraculous gifts like tongues, prophecy, and healing cease with the apostles, or do they continue today?
  • What is the New Testament gift of prophecy — infallible revelation, or Spirit-prompted speech that must be tested?
  • Were biblical tongues human languages, a heavenly prayer language, or both — and what is happening in charismatic worship today?
  • How do continuing gifts relate to the sufficiency of Scripture and a closed canon?
  • How should churches practice or restrain the gifts in corporate worship without quenching the Spirit or inviting disorder?

The Evangelical Debate

Three Positions on the Gifts of the Spirit

Evangelical conviction on the miraculous gifts runs along a spectrum, but three broad positions anchor the conversation — each claiming to take both the biblical text and the Spirit's ongoing ministry with full seriousness.

Position 1
Cessationism
John MacArthur · Richard B. Gaffin Jr. · Thomas Schreiner · Sinclair Ferguson
The miraculous and revelatory gifts served a specific redemptive-historical purpose: authenticating the apostles and prophets as they laid the church's once-for-all foundation (Eph. 2:20; Heb. 2:3-4; 2 Cor. 12:12). With that foundation laid and the canon complete, God's final word has been spoken in his Son. Since New Testament prophecy was infallible, modern claims to fallible "prophecy" redefine the biblical gift. God still heals and works miracles in answer to prayer — but the gifts, as regular endowments of the church, have ceased.

Position 2
Reformed Continuationism
Wayne Grudem · John Piper · D.A. Carson · Sam Storms
No New Testament text teaches that the gifts have ceased — and 1 Corinthians 13:8-12 places their end at Christ's return, when we see "face to face." Paul commands believers to "earnestly desire the spiritual gifts, especially that you may prophesy" (1 Cor. 14:1), a command never rescinded. Congregational prophecy is Spirit-prompted report that must be tested (1 Thess. 5:19-21), categorically distinct from the foundational apostolic word — so continuing gifts pose no threat to a closed canon. The gifts operate under Scripture's authority, tested and orderly.

Position 3
Pentecostal / Charismatic
Gordon Fee · Craig Keener · Andrew Wilson
The gifts are not merely permitted but normative for church life. Peter's Pentecost sermon defines the entire age between the Spirit's outpouring and Christ's return as the era of prophecy, visions, and Spirit-empowered witness (Acts 2:17-18). The church fathers attest that the gifts continued well past the apostles, and the global church today — where Pentecostalism numbers in the hundreds of millions — reports healing and prophecy on a scale scholars like Craig Keener have documented extensively. Every believer should expect the Spirit's manifestation for the common good (1 Cor. 12:7), exercised within Paul's ordered worship (1 Cor. 14:26-40).

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The debate has grown notably more charitable — and more precise — over the past generation. Nearly everyone now affirms that God still heals and works miracles in answer to prayer; even convinced cessationists like Schreiner say so plainly. And nearly everyone agrees that modern "prophecy" must be tested against Scripture and can never add to it; even convinced continuationists like Grudem insist on that. The remaining disagreement is real but narrower than the rhetoric of past decades suggested: whether the gifts persist as ongoing endowments of the church, and whether what charismatics call prophecy and tongues is the same phenomenon the New Testament describes. Notably, both sides now largely agree that 1 Corinthians 13 points to Christ's return rather than the canon's completion — which means the cessationist case rests on redemptive history, and the continuationist case on unrescinded commands, rather than on a single proof text.

Pastorally, the healthiest churches on every side share the same instincts: the Word governs, love outranks every gift (1 Cor. 13), order protects the congregation, and the Spirit's chief work is exalting Christ and conforming believers to him. A cessationist church that prays boldly for the sick and a charismatic church that tests every prophecy against Scripture have more in common than either has with its own caricature. Most evangelical institutions treat the question as a secondary matter — important enough to shape a congregation's worship, but not a boundary of Christian fellowship.

Frequently Asked Questions

The Holy Spirit & Spiritual Gifts, Briefly Answered

What is the difference between cessationism and continuationism?

Cessationists believe the miraculous gifts of the Spirit — especially prophecy, tongues, and healing — served to authenticate the apostles and ceased once the church's foundation was laid and the canon of Scripture was complete. Continuationists believe all the gifts of 1 Corinthians 12 remain available until Christ returns. Both camps affirm the Spirit's ongoing work in regeneration, sanctification, and illumination; the disagreement concerns the miraculous gifts specifically.

Does the Bible say the spiritual gifts have ceased?

No text explicitly says the gifts have ceased, which continuationists consider decisive. Cessationists argue instead from redemptive history: the gifts authenticated the apostles' foundational ministry (Ephesians 2:20; Hebrews 2:3-4), and with Scripture complete their purpose is fulfilled. The disputed passage is 1 Corinthians 13:8-12, where gifts pass away "when the perfect comes" — scholars on both sides now widely agree that refers to Christ's return, not the completed canon.

Is speaking in tongues for today?

It depends on whom you ask. Pentecostals and charismatics practice tongues as a Spirit-given prayer language (1 Corinthians 14:2), and many continuationists agree while denying it is required evidence of the Spirit's work. Cessationists argue biblical tongues were real human languages, as at Pentecost (Acts 2), and that modern practice does not match the biblical gift. Paul's instructions — do not forbid tongues, but require order and interpretation in public worship — frame the debate for everyone.

Do cessationists believe God still heals and does miracles?

Yes. Mainstream cessationists like Thomas Schreiner explicitly affirm that God still heals and works miracles in answer to prayer, and may grant signs and wonders in frontier missionary settings. What they deny is that individuals today possess the gift of healing or miracles as the apostles did. The disagreement is about ongoing gifted individuals, not about whether God retains and exercises supernatural power.

How should churches handle disagreement over spiritual gifts?

Most evangelical leaders treat this as a secondary issue that should not divide Christians, though it shapes worship enough that each congregation usually needs a working position. Healthy churches teach their convictions clearly while acknowledging that respected, Bible-believing scholars land on every side. Whatever the position, all agree on the tests: everything by Scripture, everything for edification, everything in order, and nothing that quenches the Spirit.

The Evangelical Conversation, Curated

1
Why I Am a Cessationist
Thomas Schreiner · The Gospel Coalition
Schreiner lays out the cessationist case with unusual warmth: the apostles and prophets laid the church's foundation (Eph. 2:20), New Testament prophecy was infallible like its Old Testament counterpart, and what charismatics call prophecy today is better described as impressions from God. He concedes 1 Corinthians 13 permits the gifts to continue — but argues Scripture and experience together verify they have not.
2
Why I Am a Continuationist
Sam Storms · The Gospel Coalition
The companion piece to Schreiner's article, written by his friend and sparring partner. Storms argues that the New Testament's portrayal of the gifts is pervasive and positive, that miracles served doxological and pastoral purposes beyond authenticating apostles, and that Paul's command to earnestly desire the gifts still stands. A model of charitable disagreement between two evangelical scholars.
3
Signs and Wonders: Then and Now
John Piper · Desiring God
Piper's classic three-part case that signs and wonders were never the exclusive property of the apostles — the seventy, Stephen, Philip, and ordinary Galatian and Corinthian believers exercised them too. Working carefully through 1 Corinthians 13:8-12, he argues the gifts are meant to continue until Jesus comes, and that neglecting them risks disobedience to a plain command.
4
Cessationism
Robert Rothwell · Tabletalk (Ligonier)
Written by a former Pentecostal turned Reformed cessationist, this Tabletalk feature explains why cessationism has been the standard Reformed position: miracles clustered around eras of new revelation, and if prophecy continues, God has not spoken finally in his Son. Rothwell is careful to insist that cessationists affirm the Spirit's ongoing power to heal and work through providence.
5
The Miraculous Gifts and the Question of Cessationism
Thomas Schreiner · The Gospel Coalition
A longer, more systematic treatment from TGC's Concise Theology series. Schreiner works through the redemptive-historical argument, the closed canon, the end of apostleship, and the hardest texts for his view — including a close reading of Agabus and Acts 21. Ends with a further-reading list spanning both sides of the debate, from Warfield and Gaffin to Grudem and Storms.
6
The Continuation of the Charismata
Andrew Wilson · Themelios
Wilson's academic opening statement in a published exchange with Schreiner and Ligon Duncan. He advances three arguments — hermeneutical (Paul's unrescinded commands), historical (the church fathers attest continuing gifts), and eschatological (the gifts characterize the whole age between Pentecost and Christ's return) — then takes on the strongest cessationist argument about infallible prophecy head-on.
7
10 Questions about Spiritual Gifts
Sam Storms · Crossway
A practical primer that defines spiritual gifts as the Spirit's manifestation for serving others, surveys the key texts, and answers questions believers actually ask: can gifts change over time, do unbelievers have them, and how do you discover yours? Storms's counsel — serve first, and your gift will find you — is useful regardless of where a reader lands on the cessationism debate.
8
Why Do We See So Few Miracles Today?
John Piper · Desiring God
Piper answers a college student's question with a corrective for both sides: there were fewer miracles in the Bible than we think — they clustered around the exodus, the prophets, and Christ — and there are more miracles today than we know. A pastoral reminder that Christian faith rests on the finished work of Christ, not on the miracle we saw yesterday.
