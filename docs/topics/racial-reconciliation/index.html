Race, Justice, and the Church

The evangelical conversation on racial reconciliation—what the gospel demands, where the church has failed, and what faithful witness looks like today.

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Few issues test the evangelical conscience more deeply than racial justice. The American evangelical church has a complicated and painful history with race. Many white evangelical congregations supported slavery or remained silent during Jim Crow. White evangelicals largely resisted the Civil Rights Movement, choosing to remain quiet when the gospel demanded prophetic witness. Black evangelicals carried the gospel faithfully through centuries of oppression and injustice, often feeling invisible in majority-white evangelical structures. That history casts a long shadow, and it shapes how different evangelical communities approach the question of racial reconciliation today. What becomes clear when we reckon with this history is that racial reconciliation is not a peripheral social issue but a gospel issue. If Christ’s death broke down the dividing wall of hostility between Jew and Gentile, creating one new humanity, then a church divided by race contradicts the message it proclaims. How we treat people of different races is not a second-order question of personal preference; it is a fundamental question of Christian faithfulness.

The current evangelical conversation on race is marked by significant common ground and significant division. Nearly all evangelicals now affirm that racial prejudice is sin and that the dignity of every person flows from their creation in God’s image. Evangelicals across the board condemn explicit racism and believe the gospel calls us to love our neighbors, including our neighbors of different races. Where evangelicals disagree is on the nature and scope of structural injustice, on how to address inequities that outlast the legal elimination of Jim Crow, on the language we use to talk about these problems, and on the urgency with which the church should prioritize racial justice work. Some evangelical leaders worry that an emphasis on systemic racism diminishes individual moral responsibility or that certain justice frameworks are more political than theological. Others argue that ignoring structural sin and systemic inequity is itself a form of injustice. These disagreements are real, but they should not paralyze us from moving forward in repentance and reconciliation.

What matters most is that the evangelical church begin to see racial justice not as an optional cause for the culturally progressive but as integral to the gospel itself. This means repenting of our complicity in systemic racism. It means listening to the voices of Black evangelicals and other evangelicals of color who have long been marginalized in majority-white spaces. It means examining our own churches: Does our membership reflect the diversity of our communities? Does our leadership? Are our sermons and teaching informed by voices of color? Do our budgets and resources reflect a commitment to justice? And it means recognizing that reconciliation is not a project we complete but a lifelong posture of humility, repentance, and solidarity with those who have been wounded by racial injustice. For this work to be authentic, it must be rooted in the conviction that Christ is reconciling all things to himself, that his kingdom transcends all human divisions, and that the church is called to embody that reconciliation in the present.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The evangelical conversation on racial justice reveals both a crisis and an opportunity. The crisis is that the American evangelical church, particularly its white majority, has failed to be a prophetic voice on racial issues at critical historical moments. From slavery through Jim Crow to the present, white evangelicals have often chosen silence, neutrality, or even opposition to racial justice, citing spiritual concerns or the separation of faith from politics. This failure has wounded the witness of the church, contributed to the marginalization of Black evangelicals in predominantly white evangelical spaces, and positioned the church as a defender of the status quo rather than as an agent of God’s liberating justice. The opportunity is that more and more evangelical leaders are acknowledging this failure, confessing it, and asking how the gospel demands a different response. The emerging evangelical consensus—still contested, but growing—is that racial justice is not a political issue to be avoided but a gospel issue that the church must address with urgency and faithfulness.

What the best evangelical voices on racial reconciliation teach us is that this work is both theological and practical. Theologically, we must recover the biblical insistence that all human beings bear God’s image and that Christ’s reconciling work extends to all people. Practically, we must examine the structures of our churches—our membership requirements, our leadership development, our preaching, our budget allocation—to see whether they reflect a genuine commitment to unity across racial lines. We must listen to Black evangelicals and other evangelicals of color, not as consultants but as leaders in our own right. We must confess the complicity of previous generations and our own ongoing complicity in systems of racial injustice. And we must commit ourselves to the long, difficult, Spirit-empowered work of becoming churches in which all races can belong fully and lead freely. This is what faithful witness looks like in a moment when the world is watching to see whether Christianity has any power to heal the deepest wounds of human society.
