Best Christian Articles on Discipleship

What does it actually mean to make disciples — and why is the local church the irreplaceable context for it?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

Jesus did not commission his followers to make converts; he commissioned them to make disciples — people who learn to obey everything he commanded, in every dimension of life. That distinction carries enormous practical weight. Conversion is an event; discipleship is a lifelong process of formation, and it requires intentional relationship, sustained community, and patient instruction over time.

Evangelical Christianity has not always been clear on this. The tradition's emphasis on personal conversion, combined with the rise of individualistic spiritual practice, has sometimes produced Christians who understand salvation but have little experience of what it means to be deliberately formed by a more mature believer. The result is churches full of people who made a decision but were never discipled into the fullness of Christian life.

The best evangelical thinking on discipleship is recovering something the New Testament assumes throughout: that Christian formation happens in relationship, within the structure of the local church, and through the ordinary means of God's grace — the Word, prayer, the Lord's Supper, and the body of Christ. The articles below represent the sharpest thinking on what it means to disciple and be discipled.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What is the difference between evangelism and discipleship — and why does it matter?
  • What does the New Testament actually show us about how discipleship worked in the early church?
  • How does a church cultivate a culture of intentional disciple-making rather than just program attendance?
  • What is the role of one-on-one relationships in spiritual formation?
  • How do small groups function as discipleship contexts rather than just social communities?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
Discipleship According to the Scriptures
· 9Marks
The essential biblical foundation piece. This article argues that discipleship doesn't just happen — it requires intentional cultivation of deep, honest relationships in which believers deliberately do spiritual good to one another. It traces the pattern of discipleship through the New Testament and shows why it is inherently relational, not merely programmatic.
2
Disciple Others
· 9Marks
A practical piece on what it means to deliberately help another person grow in conformity to Jesus. Discipling is defined here not as a program but as an intentional relationship — meeting regularly, reading Scripture together, praying, and speaking honestly into one another's lives. Clear, actionable, and grounded in what the New Testament actually shows.
3
Grow a Disciple-Making Culture in Your Church
· The Gospel Coalition
Addresses the institutional challenge: how does a church move from a program-centric model to a culture where disciple-making is the normal expectation of every member? The article defines disciple-making as an intentional process of evangelizing non-believers, establishing believers in faith, and equipping leaders — and outlines the pastoral moves that shift a church's culture toward this end.
4
Your Small Group Should Be Making Disciples
· The Gospel Coalition
Small groups are the most common discipleship structure in evangelical churches — but many function as social communities or Bible information sessions rather than genuine disciple-making environments. This article clarifies what has to change: shared accountability, intentional growth goals, and the church community as the context for genuine spiritual transformation.
5
The Four Causes of Spiritual Formation
· The Gospel Coalition Canada
A theologically rich piece drawing on Aristotle's four causes to analyze what actually produces spiritual formation. What is the material of spiritual growth? What is its formal structure? What moves it? What is its goal? The Aristotelian framework is a vehicle for a genuinely illuminating account of how the Holy Spirit works through ordinary means to transform ordinary people.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The thread running through all of these articles is a recovery of what discipleship actually looks like in the New Testament: intentional, relational, and embedded in the local church. It is not a program you attend; it is a pattern of life you enter with others. The early church devoted itself to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer — and those four activities are still the structure within which genuine Christian formation happens.

The practical implication for churches is a shift from attraction to formation: from asking 'how do we get people to come?' to 'how do we help the people who come to become like Christ?' These are different questions, and they require different kinds of investment — less platform, more relationship; less event, more sustained presence.
