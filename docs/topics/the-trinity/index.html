On the Trinity

What does it mean that God is one being in three persons — and why does Trinitarian theology matter for prayer, salvation, and everyday Christian life?

Last updated: April 17, 2026

TL;DR

God is one being in three persons means the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit share one divine nature while maintaining distinct personal identities. Trinitarian theology matters because Christians pray to the Father through the Son by the Spirit, salvation involves all three persons, and Christian community reflects God's relational nature.

The Trinity is perhaps the most distinctive Christian conviction about God, and yet it may be the least understood or practiced in evangelical piety. Affirmed in creeds, sung in hymns, and taught in seminaries, it often remains abstract and removed from lived faith. Yet the doctrine is not a medieval luxury or obscure technical achievement; it shapes how we understand prayer (addressing Father, Son, and Spirit), salvation (the work of all three persons), worship (singing the Gloria Patri), and the very nature of love and community (since God is eternally relational within Himself).

In recent years, evangelical theology has witnessed serious debate about how to articulate Trinitarian doctrine. Are the persons 'eternally subordinate' in function? Or do they possess full equality? How do we preserve both God's absolute oneness and the genuine threeness of persons? These aren't abstract scholastic games; they affect how we preach about Christ's obedience, how we understand the Spirit's role in the church, and how we address cultural questions about gender roles. Getting the Trinity right matters because getting it wrong can distort our understanding of who God is and what He has accomplished.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses

  • How are God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit one being yet three distinct persons?
  • Is the traditional language of 'substance' and 'persons' the best framework for expressing Trinitarian truth?
  • Are the three persons eternally equal in attributes, authority, and honor?
  • What does it mean that Jesus submitted to the Father, and does this imply eternal subordination?
  • How does the Trinity affect Christian experience: prayer, worship, community, and the image of God in humanity?

The Evangelical Debate

Three Frameworks for Understanding the Trinity

Contemporary evangelical theology has produced three major ways of framing Trinitarian doctrine, each attempting to preserve the classical conviction of one being in three persons while articulating it in ways that address modern philosophical challenges and pastoral concerns.

Position 1
Classical Trinitarianism
Sinclair Ferguson, Alister McGrath, Robert Letham, Gregg Allison
God is one divine substance (ousia) eternally subsisting in three distinct persons (hypostaseis): Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The persons are fully equal in deity, power, and eternality, yet possess distinct subsistences and economic roles. The Son's incarnation and submission were voluntary acts in time, not expressions of eternal subordination. This framework preserves biblical language and classical Christian tradition while protecting both God's absolute unity and the real personhood and distinctness of Father, Son, and Spirit.
Key Reads
The Christian God: Essential Elements of Christian Theology Trinity and Covenant
Position 2
Evangelical Tritheism / Soft Subordinationism
Wayne Grudem, Tom Schreiner, Randy Alcorn, Paul Helm
God eternally subsists as Father, Son, and Spirit in a relationship of voluntary, functional subordination of the Son and Spirit to the Father. The Son is eternally the Son (not begotten at the incarnation but eternally generated) and eternally submits to the Father's headship while remaining fully divine and worthy of worship. This view claims to do better justice to biblical passages about submission and subordination while maintaining the deity of all three persons, grounding it in an understanding of eternal hierarchical headship compatible with divine equality.
Key Reads
Evangelical Feminism: A New Path to Biblicism? The Mystery of the Trinity
Position 3
Social Trinitarianism
Miroslav Volf, Paul McPartlan, John Zizioulas, Leonardo Boff
The Trinity is best understood as three divine persons in eternal community and mutual indwelling (perichoresis), each fully divine and each freely relating to the others. God's nature is fundamentally relational and social; the Trinity provides the archetype for human community, reconciliation, and the church. This framework emphasizes the threeness over the oneness, grounds the Trinity in relationality rather than substance, and offers rich theological resources for thinking about human personhood, community, and justice as reflections of God's Trinitarian life.

What the Conversation Adds Up To

The Trinitarian debate reveals how difficult it is to talk about God at the limits of language and reason. Classical trinitarianism rightly insists on God's absolute simplicity and the full equality of persons; subordinationism rightly points to biblical texts about headship and submission; social trinitarianism rightly emphasizes relationality and community as reflections of God's nature. Yet each framework struggles with tensions the others expose. The healthiest evangelical approach may be to hold classical Trinitarian affirmations (one being, three persons, full equality) while remaining humble about how to systematize the doctrine and remaining pastoral about its implications for church life and spiritual experience.

Ultimately, the Trinity is not a puzzle to solve but a mystery to worship. It tells us that God is eternally other (transcendent) and eternally relational (immanent), that oneness does not require uniformity, and that the deepest reality of God is communion. For evangelicals, the question is not whether to abandon classical Trinitarian doctrine but how to make it live in prayer, preaching, and community — how to pray to the Father through the Son by the Spirit, how to find in the Trinity the archetype of Christian unity and mission, and how to let this central mystery reshape our understanding of what it means to be made in the image of God.
