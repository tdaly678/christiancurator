Best Christian Articles on Vocation & Work

What does it mean to work as a Christian — and how does the Reformation doctrine of vocation transform Monday morning?

Curated by Christian Curator · Updated regularly

One of the great contributions of the Protestant Reformation was the recovery of vocation — the conviction that ordinary work is not a distraction from the spiritual life but a primary arena for it. Against the medieval hierarchy that elevated monastery over marketplace and priest over plowman, Luther insisted that the plowman who does his work faithfully to the glory of God is engaged in a genuinely holy calling. This was a revolutionary idea in the sixteenth century, and it remains countercultural in our own.

The doctrine of vocation holds that every legitimate form of human work — not just 'full-time ministry' — is a calling from God through which he provides for his creatures and through which Christians can love their neighbors and glorify their Creator — the same motive that drives missions and evangelism. The factory worker, the teacher, the accountant, the parent at home — all are exercising a vocation in the Reformation sense. The secular/sacred distinction that haunts so much evangelical consciousness is not a biblical category.

The evangelical recovery of vocation theology in the past generation — associated especially with Gene Veith, Tim Keller, and the Faith & Work movement — has given Christians a framework for thinking about the forty or fifty hours most of them spend working each week. That framework is not primarily about using your workplace as an evangelism platform; it is about understanding that the work itself, done faithfully and skillfully, is an act of worship and service. The articles below represent the best of this conversation.

Key Questions This Topic Addresses
  • What is the Reformation doctrine of vocation — and how does it differ from how most evangelicals think about calling?
  • Is all legitimate work a calling from God, or is 'vocation' reserved for ministry and certain professions?
  • How does the theology of vocation transform the way a Christian approaches their ordinary job?
  • What is the relationship between vocation and the Great Commission — is work itself missional?
  • How should Christians think about unemployment, career change, and unfulfilling work through a vocational lens?
The Evangelical Conversation, Curated
1
The Doctrine of Vocation
· The Gospel Coalition
The essential reference essay on vocation theology. Drawing on Luther's recovery of the concept and its development in Reformed thought, this piece explains why vocation is 'one of the greatest — though strangely neglected and forgotten — teachings of the Reformation.' The argument: God is hidden in vocation, working through ordinary human activity to provide for creation and love his creatures — which means ordinary work is never merely ordinary.
2
The Sacred Call to Normal Work
· Desiring God
A historical and theological piece tracing the Reformation recovery of work's dignity through the Puritans and into the evangelical tradition. The Puritans understood normal work — farming, trading, keeping house — as a sacred trust and holy privilege, not a distraction from spiritual life. This piece retrieves that conviction and applies it to contemporary Christians who struggle to find meaning in their day-to-day labor.
3
What Are You Called to Do? A Theology of Work
· The Gospel Coalition
A practical and accessible treatment of how vocation theology applies to real career decisions and daily work. 'You work because it glorifies God' — but what does that mean Monday morning in a cubicle? This piece translates the theological principle into livable terms: excellence, integrity, service to neighbor, and a refusal to let work become an idol or an afterthought.
4
How Vocation Brings Dignity to Your Work
· The Gospel Coalition
The doctrine of vocation is not only theologically significant — it is psychologically transformative. When a Christian understands that their work is a calling through which God provides for their neighbors and is glorified in creation, the work itself takes on a different character. This article explores how vocation theology is an antidote to the meaninglessness that afflicts so much contemporary work.
5
You Already Work a Christian Job
· The Gospel Coalition
A liberating corrective to the idea that some jobs are inherently more spiritual than others. The article argues that the category distinction between 'Christian work' (ministry, missions) and 'secular work' (everything else) is not a biblical category — and that recovering the Reformation doctrine of vocation means recognizing that any lawful work done to the glory of God is Christian work.
What the Conversation Adds Up To

The doctrine of vocation is one of those theological recoveries that transforms daily life. When a Christian understands that their Monday morning work is not a distraction from their spiritual life but an expression of it — that God is hidden in their vocation, loving their neighbors through their hands — the character of ordinary labor changes. Boredom, frustration, and meaninglessness remain real, but they are now interpreted within a framework that gives them a different significance.

The deepest implication of vocation theology is that the church does not need to 'make work spiritual' by turning every workplace into an evangelism platform. The work itself — done faithfully, skillfully, and honestly — is already spiritual. It is service to the neighbor, provision for creation, and participation in God's ongoing care for the world. That is more than enough reason to do it well.
