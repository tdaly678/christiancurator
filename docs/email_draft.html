The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what holds a congregation together when its center of gravity shifts, whether that means a pastor's fall, a confused eschatology, or a Bible that feels distant. Jamie Strickler at The Christian Post focuses on the congregation left behind after a celebrity pastor's removal: the sanctuary looks the same, but no one knows whether to say his name. The piece is less about the fallen leader than about the ecclesial wound, who tends it, who even sees it. Travis Hearne's review at The Gospel Coalition argues that congregations without historical memory are more vulnerable to exactly that kind of collapse, because they have no model of the church that outlasts its current personality.

Kim Dang, also at The Gospel Coalition, pushes the question inward: when Scripture feels dead, it is usually because readers are tracking themes instead of a Person, and no congregational structure fixes that. Michael Bird's essay on eschatology adds a different angle, warning that when Christians treat mission as a mechanism to accelerate the Second Coming, they have replaced faithfulness with a project, the same displacement Strickler worries about in celebrity culture. New data from Religion News Service complicates the picture further: Gen Z men and women are arriving at faith through different doors, men through identity and attendance, women through interior life, which means a congregation rebuilding after crisis may need to ask which kind of belonging it has actually been offering. November 13's dispatch watched a similar tension surface around authority and apostolic witness; today the same question is arriving through the pastoral door.
