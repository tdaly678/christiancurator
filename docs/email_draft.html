The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing hard this week on a single uncomfortable question: how many people sitting in churches actually possess the faith they profess? Tim Challies argues that a reduced gospel produces a reduced response, leaving people confident in religious experience rather than in Christ himself. Timothy Paul Jones, presenting at the Evangelical Theological Society, takes the question ecclesially: the visible life of the church, he contends, is itself an apologetic, and a thin church makes a thin case. These are two different diagnoses of the same problem. Challies locates the failure in what gets preached; Jones locates it in what gets embodied.

Mitch Chase's close reading of John 6:40 gives the question its sharpest edge: Jesus promises resurrection specifically to those who believe, which raises the stakes for whether belief is real. Michael Bird's essay on inspiration and pseudonymous authorship sits nearby in the conversation, arguing that divine authority does not depend on apostolic signature, a move that defends the canon's credibility without flattening the historical questions. Nijay Gupta's survey of Tom Holland and Peter Williams on Gospel reliability adds another layer: confident historical defense of the texts is part of what makes genuine faith possible, not merely pious. Ryan Burge's data on Gen Alpha shows the cost of the gap from outside: four in ten non-religious teenagers have no one to talk to, while Christian teens report parents, grandparents, and pastors. Earlier this week we were watching formation and desire circle the same ground. The question hasn't changed, only sharpened: what does it take to move someone from profession to possession, and does the church know how to do that anymore.
