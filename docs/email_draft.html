The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what it means to be human in an age when machines are being asked to perform the work of souls. Rev. Kevin Deal, writing in The Guardian, argues that Silicon Valley's search for artificial souls is not a technical problem but a philosophical one: humans experience beauty, sit with mystery, and exist for more than utility, and no language model changes that. The Religion News Service piece on scholar D.Z. Kalman comes at the same question from the other direction, asking not what AI threatens but what faith might actually offer to technology's architects. Samuel D. James, writing for Crossway, pulls the frame further back: the biblical sluggard, he argues, is the patron figure of the digital age, someone who wants to receive without cultivating, and technology makes that posture easier to sustain and harder to name.

James Eglinton, writing for The Gospel Coalition, supplies the theological architecture underneath all three pieces: secularism has not escaped the need for ultimate meaning, it has only borrowed it from religion while pretending not to, and that contradiction is why modern life feels unlivable to so many people. That question of what secularism actually delivers was also moving through the conversation back in November when writers were tracing how technology concentrates power and how the church ought to think about that. Mitch Chase, working through John 6 on his Substack, adds the exegetical grounding: the disciples' failure to understand Jesus is not a comprehension problem but a spiritual one, and that distinction matters when the church is tempted to treat the formation of human persons as an engineering task. The writers here are not all saying the same thing about technology, but they are asking the same question about what kind of creature a human being is, and whether that answer still has teeth.
