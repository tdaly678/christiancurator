The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what faithful church membership actually requires, and the answers are pulling in different directions. David Mathis at Desiring God argues that covenant commitment is not a burden but the specific remedy God designed for anxious, unmoored Christians: the very thing people resist is what stabilizes them. The Gospel Coalition's Matt Smethurst and Ligon Duncan push the question outward, arguing that a committed congregation is also an evangelizing one, and that pastors model this by making gospel conversations ordinary rather than exceptional. Nora Allison, also at The Gospel Coalition, adds that women's Bible study is a site where this formation actually happens, and that churches are failing to train enough women to lead it. The practical shape of membership, it turns out, is inseparable from the question of who teaches, who evangelizes, and who stays.

Tim Challies names one of the quieter tensions in this: a church member who disagrees with leadership's worship choices has a commitment problem as much as a taste problem, and Challies walks through what staying in good conscience looks like. The Calvary Chapel Costa Mesa ordination dispute, covered in The Christian Post, shows how sharp these membership questions become when the disagreement is doctrinal: pastors across the movement have issued public opposition to Char Brodersen's decision to ordain women, and the question of who belongs to what kind of church is now institutional, not merely personal. Cole Mizel at The Gospel Coalition adds a quieter footnote, arguing that the evangelical habit of morning quiet times is more cultural inheritance than biblical mandate, which is a small but telling example of how practices get mistaken for commitments. Earlier this week we were watching a similar question surface around desire and formation: whether the habits Christians hold most tightly are actually shaping them, or simply marking them. The writers this week suggest the church is still working out the difference.
