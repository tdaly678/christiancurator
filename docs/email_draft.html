The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what faithful witness looks like when the church shows up where institutions have failed or retreated. In Burundi, Relevant Magazine's Margaret Philbrick reports, local churches reached vaccine-hesitant girls after rumors drove them away from health workers entirely. Christianity Today's Tonny Onyulo profiles Christian leaders in eastern Congo going door to door during an Ebola outbreak, persuading families to seek treatment in a climate of fear and mistrust. Both pieces describe the church not as a supplement to public health infrastructure but as the only credible presence left. Sho Baraka, writing for Christianity Today, turns the same logic toward Silicon Valley's margins, visiting a nonprofit that teaches low-income youth in East Palo Alto the technical skills that the surrounding economy withholds from them.

Sacha Mugisha at Mere Orthodoxy sharpens the theological question underneath all of this, drawing a line between Christian vitality, life rooted in resurrection and gift, and what he calls Nietzschean vitalism, strength performing itself. The distinction matters for mission: a church that serves Congo families or Burundian schoolgirls because it has something to give looks very different from one that serves to demonstrate its own relevance. Kevin DeYoung at Crossway supplies the grounding: Christian witness is not philosophical self-help but testimony to actual events, which means it must land in actual places with actual people. Earlier this week we were watching a parallel argument about mission and witness unfold around the question of what legitimate authority looks like in public life. The current pieces move that question closer to the ground, where the authority being tested is simply a congregation's willingness to go.
