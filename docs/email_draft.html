Evangelical writers are pressing hard this week on the question of what legitimate authority looks like, in Scripture, in politics, and in the pew. At The Gospel Coalition, Michael Kruger argues that the full divinity of Jesus was not a fourth-century invention but a conviction held from the beginning, which matters because the church's doctrinal authority rests on historical continuity, not conciliar novelty. Kruger extends that argument at Word First, contending that non-apostolic New Testament books still carry apostolic authority because they were authorized and endorsed by the apostolic circle. The canon, on this reading, is not a power grab by later bishops but the apostles' own legacy. That is a tighter account of authority than many assume.

The political question is thornier. Peter Leithart at First Things reads Samuel's case against monarchy as a biblical theology of power, where even legitimate rule tends toward domination and Israel's deeper calling is to lovingkindness rather than statecraft. The ERLC's Baptist case against Christian nationalism lands in a similar place: the church witnesses to the kingdom by remaining distinct from the state, not by capturing it. Mere Orthodoxy presses the same logic into Silicon Valley, arguing that AI development is catechized by power and contempt for democratic accountability, values that Christians should recognize and name. Meanwhile, at Christianity Today, Gen Z delegates at a global conference are finding in the Lausanne Covenant not a political program but a missionary one, which is its own answer to where the church's authority should aim. Earlier this week we were watching the tension between urgency and faithfulness surface in formation debates; here it surfaces in constitutional ones, with writers broadly agreeing that the church's power is most itself when it refuses to look like the world's.
