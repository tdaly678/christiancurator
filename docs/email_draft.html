The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on whether Christianity's shrinking cultural footprint is a crisis to be managed or a clarifying moment for the church's witness. Pew Research and Ryan Burge's data tell the same story from different angles: Islam is projected to outnumber Christianity globally by 2035, and Australian Protestantism has collapsed faster than even the American variety. The numbers matter, but the more interesting debate is over what the church is actually offering a secular age. James Eglinton at The Gospel Coalition argues that secularism isn't replacing Christianity with nothing: it has its own competing ultimates, its own functional monotheism, and that tension is precisely why modernity feels unlivable to so many people.

The question of what the church offers runs straight into arguments about how it presents itself. John Ehrett at First Things describes a faction of the American right shedding Christian substance while keeping Christian cultural energy, a move Ehrett treats as a coherent response to electoral losses but a theological dead end. Logan Tantibanchachai at Mere Orthodoxy comes at it differently, arguing that the Christian right's real deficit is not strategy but mercy. Mitch Chase's essay on John 6 adds a sharper edge: Jesus offended his own disciples, and a church that smooths that offense to stay culturally legible has lost what it was supposed to offer. That question of a costly, undiluted witness hasn't gone away; earlier this week writers were working the same tension from the angle of canon and apostolic authority. The data about declining numbers may finally be less urgent than the argument about whether the church knows what it is.
