The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what conversion actually is and who does the work. Gregg Allison at 9Marks argues that repentance is not something a person generates: the Spirit prompts it, full stop, because human hostility to grace is too stubborn to be overcome by human will alone. David Schuman at The Gospel Coalition arrives at a similar place from a different direction, locating the Christian's hope not in the sincerity of repentance but in the compassion of God. Both pieces are pushing against a subtle moralism: the idea that conversion is secured by doing repentance correctly. Where they differ is in emphasis, Allison focusing on what the Spirit initiates, Schuman on what God's character guarantees.

Building Jerusalem complicates the picture by asking whether anyone else can verify the result: Christians, it argues, cannot reliably judge another person's faith, given that self-deception is real and wolves do exist. That is not skepticism about conversion; it is a check on the confidence any observer can claim. O. Alan Noble adds a pastoral register, arguing that doubt during a mental health crisis does not require losing faith, which implies that the Spirit's work can persist through states that look, from the outside, like its absence. Russell Moore presses the practical edge: genuine grace, he writes this week, cannot simply move past past wrongs without confronting them, suggesting that real repentance has an ongoing, backward-facing shape. Taken together, these writers are not debating whether conversion happens. They are arguing about what it looks like from the inside, from the outside, and across time.
