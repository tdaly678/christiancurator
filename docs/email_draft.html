The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are asking what the church owes the public square after an election, and whether its witness there is coherent. Daniel Darling calls the moment one for reflection and renewed witness, arguing that Christians should resist the temptation to read electoral outcomes as either vindication or defeat. At American Reformer, Nathanael Devlin pushes harder: the church has a prophetic responsibility to speak to the nations, not retreat into private piety. The gap between those two positions is real. Darling wants sobriety and long-term faithfulness; Devlin wants the church to stop treating public silence as a virtue.

Dominic Bouck at First Things brings the sharpest edge, noting that when Trump admitted to Ross Douthat he cannot forgive his enemies, it raised a question about what Christian witness in political life actually requires of those who invoke it. That question was alive last week too, when writers were pressing on what legitimate authority looks like in a Christian frame. At 9Marks, Phill Howell's review of Brad East's book on the church argues that the congregation, not the political order, is the primary community where Christian identity is formed, which puts a quiet check on both Darling's caution and Devlin's ambition. Kyle Hughes at Mere Orthodoxy extends the question into education, warning that classical Christian schools drift toward the conventional when they forget what makes their witness distinct. Formation, not electoral results, is where most of these writers end up.
