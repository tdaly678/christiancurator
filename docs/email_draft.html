The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what Christian identity actually holds together when outside forces, political, tribal, and cultural, keep pulling it apart. The ERLC's Amanda Hays addresses pastors directly in the wake of the midterms, arguing that the congregation's primary loyalty must be to Christ before any party, and that the pastor's job after an election is harder than before it. Desiring God frames the same pressure in discipleship terms: tribalism trains people to conform to us, while genuine formation aims at Christ, and the two are not compatible goals dressed in different clothes. Both pieces are asking whether the church is forming people into something, or just reflecting what they already are.

Russell Moore at Christianity Today pulls the question somewhere more personal. Writing from his newsletter, Moore observes that faith does not automatically dissolve trauma, and that expecting it to can itself become a kind of spiritual pressure that isolates the wounded. That concern connects to what The Christian Post's Jerry McGlothlin argues about two deficient extremes: the prosperity reading that promises God wants you healthy and happy, and a grim stoicism that makes suffering the whole story. Neither, he says, is what Scripture actually holds. Jen Wilkin, Courtney Doctor, and Melissa Kruger at The Gospel Coalition bring the question down to a family level, suggesting that caring well for aging parents requires the same theological honesty about frailty and dignity that caring for the very young does. Earlier this week we were watching formation and desire converge around similar questions about whether the church's practices actually shape people for the life it proclaims. The writers here are less concerned with getting the doctrine right in the abstract than with whether it holds when someone is sitting across the table from you, grieving or aging or simply confused about what their faith is supposed to do.
