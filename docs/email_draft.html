The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers this week are pressing on what it means to trust God when what he gives, he also takes away, and what faithful gratitude looks like in the gap. The Gospel Coalition's Daylan Woodall reads Job as a case study in exactly this: Job loses the gifts but not the giver, and the lesson is that God's love is not coextensive with his provision. Mitch Chase at his Substack pushes the same point from a different angle, arguing that gratitude is a spiritual discipline that must be exercised precisely when it feels least natural. Gary Thomas draws on Richard Baxter to press further still: thanksgiving oriented toward gifts is good, but praise oriented toward God's character is a higher register of the same movement, one that survives when the gifts are absent.

Seth Lewis captures the pastoral version of this question in a coffee-shop conversation: does God actually care about one small planet, one small life? His answer is that the incarnation is the evidence. That answer connects directly to what Julia Higgins argues at The Gospel Coalition: Jesus is not only the Savior of human beings but the definition of what it means to image God well, which means human life carries a weight that makes the question of divine care almost inevitable. Paul David Tripp at Crossway brings it to the family table, arguing that teaching children about Christ's righteousness is itself an act of gratitude, an acknowledgment that our best efforts fall short and that the gift we most need has already been given. Taken together, these writers are not so much debating gratitude as they are triangulating it: gratitude for things, praise for the giver, and wonder at the fact that the giver noticed us at all.
