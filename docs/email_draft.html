The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what faithful witness looks like when the Christian message meets active resistance, whether from a culture that smiles while it excludes, from teachers who distort the gospel, or from states that imprison believers for it. Casey McCall argues that American hostility to Christian faith rarely announces itself as hostility: it arrives wearing the face of tolerance, reframing Christian conviction as bigotry so gradually that many believers never notice the shift. John Stonestreet and Glenn Sunshine at The Christian Post name a different kind of threat from inside: Joel Webbon's Christian nationalism, they argue, is not a faithful response to cultural pressure but an idolatrous one, fusing the gospel to race realism and ethnic hierarchy in ways Scripture forbids. Mark Hallock adds a pastoral register to that concern, writing that false teaching is most dangerous when the teacher is winsome and the error is slow to surface. These three pieces are not making the same argument, but they share a diagnosis: the church's discernment is being tested from multiple directions at once.

Dane Ortlund at 9Marks suggests the corrective runs through preaching itself, specifically whether Christ is diffused through the whole of a sermon or only visited at the end as a footnote. That question connects to the Roys Report's account of Francine Neff, whose faith-healing theology, shaped by Andrew Wommack's teaching, led her to delay cancer treatment until it was too late: a case where doctrine carried real, mortal weight. BBC News is reporting this week that hundreds of North Korean defectors describe imprisonment and torture for Christian faith, which puts the Western conversation about cultural pressure in a different proportion without dismissing it. Nijay Gupta, reviewing Pricelis Perreaux-Dominguez's work, argues that honest witness includes naming weakness and pain rather than projecting invulnerability, a posture the false-teacher pieces implicitly require but rarely model. The church's argument this week is not only about who is attacking it from outside, but about whether its own voice is clear, grounded, and honest enough to be trusted.
