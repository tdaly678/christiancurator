The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what faithful ministry requires of the person doing it, whether preacher, missionary, or pastor, and the answers keep circling back to the same word: seriousness. Marshall Segal at Desiring God argues that shorter attention spans are tempting preachers toward gimmicks, and that the remedy is not a better technique but a congregation that has learned to receive the word as sufficient. Tom Sugimura, writing from his Substack, grounds that claim in Paul's charge to Timothy: the word saves, and the minister's job is to continue in it, not to supplement it. Matt Bennett and Ryan Robertson at Crossway press the same logic toward missionaries specifically, warning that a high sense of calling can quietly become a reason to treat personal sin leniently, which is precisely where formation breaks down. The ministry and the minister are not separable questions.

Matt Rusten at The Gospel Coalition adds a praxis edge: preaching that ignores the five days between Sundays fails the people sitting in the pew, and the New Testament actually supplies two distinct frameworks for addressing workers that most pastors leave unused. Daniel Darling at Lifeway Research makes a related argument from the formation side, contending that discipleship stalls when churches treat the marketplace as outside their jurisdiction. Cornelis Venema at Ligonier brings a doctrinal pressure point to the whole conversation: if assurance of salvation has eroded in evangelical churches, as he argues it has, then the minister who does not preach it clearly is leaving people without the confidence that makes bold obedience possible in the first place. The theme connecting all of these is not competence but integrity, the gap between what ministry claims to do and what the minister actually believes about the word he is handling.
