The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what gratitude actually is, whether it is a felt experience, a moral discipline, or something the church forms in people over time. Pavlos Papadopoulos at First Things argues that gratitude is constitutively familial: it is learned at a table, across generations, and cannot be abstracted from the particular bodies and histories that share it. Josh Squires at Ligonier starts from a different angle, reading Psalm 117 as evidence that gratitude is the heart's proper response to grace, not a family practice but a theological posture that precedes any setting. The two pieces are not in conflict exactly, but they locate gratitude differently: one in formation, one in grace.

Laura Cerbus at Mere Orthodoxy pulls the thread tighter by naming what gratitude is set against. Greed, she writes, cannot be redirected or harnessed; it must be killed. That is a harder claim than most Thanksgiving-season writing will make, and it sets up the formation question sharply: if gratitude is the alternative to greed, it cannot be merely a mood. Jake Meador, also at Mere Orthodoxy, recovers Wolfgang Musculus on Psalm 23 to make a related point: the soul's vitality dries up unless it withdraws periodically from worldly affairs, which is to say, gratitude requires a kind of ascesis, a practiced turning away. The A Life Overseas essay on missionary kids adds a praxis edge: children formed by communities that mistake performance for faithfulness come home deflated, not grateful. Taken together, these writers suggest the church has a formation problem, not just a feelings problem, when it comes to thankfulness.
