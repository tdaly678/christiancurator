The Daily Pulse

Evangelical writers are arguing this week about what Christian faithfulness actually requires of a person, and the disagreement runs from exegesis all the way down to church practice. Scot McKnight is pressing on what Paul even meant by a Christian life, pointing out that the question remains genuinely disputed and that assuming a settled answer has real costs. Desiring God adds texture to that by revisiting the tears of Jesus at Lazarus's tomb, arguing that Jesus wept not over death itself but over the unbelief surrounding it, which shifts what his emotions model for us. Hans Madueme at Crossway is working a related angle: miracle belief is not the same category of claim as flat-earth denial, because God designed human senses to reliably engage a natural order that miracles interrupt, not disprove. The doctrinal stakes in all three pieces are the same: what Christians confess about Jesus shapes how they read their own experience, their grief, their doubt, and their reason.

The praxis side of the conversation is just as busy. The Gospel Coalition profiles a 74-member church that trained five laymen to preach expository sermons so their pastor could take a sabbatical, a small story with a clear argument inside it: the congregation, not just the professional ministry, bears the weight of faithfulness. Lifeway Research finds that church, family, and smartphones all shaped evangelical young adults' faith, roughly in that order, which means formation is happening whether churches design it or not. Relevant Magazine's Megan Buss writes about adult children who believe differently than their parents, naming the relational cost quietly absorbed in many evangelical households. That question hasn't gone away: earlier this year we watched writers argue over what transformation actually looks like and whether it's real, and Buss's piece suggests the fracture lines often appear inside families before they appear anywhere else. A. Trevor Sutton at Christianity Today closes the loop by returning to Philippians 4:8, reading it not as a retreat from difficulty but as a command to fix attention on what is genuinely excellent, which is its own answer to the question of how formation survives pressure.
