March 21, 2026

The conversation threading through so much of what we're reading today is deceptively simple: what does faithfulness actually look like when the world is loud and moving fast? Christianity Today's piece Urgency Is Not Faithfulness puts it plainly — the pressure to react, to perform conviction in real time, is not the same thing as genuine fidelity to Christ. And yet the Gospel Coalition is sounding a different kind of alarm, warning that false gospels aren't staying online — they're moving into our small groups, our neighborhoods, our families. These two concerns aren't contradictory; they're actually the same coin. The church is wrestling with a deep ecclesiological and epistemological question: who do we trust to form us, and how do we tell the difference between urgency that is prophetic and urgency that is simply anxious?

There's a quieter thread running alongside that one, and I find it genuinely moving. On one side, American Reformer's piece on fracture names the sociological reality honestly — we are a fragmented church in a fragmented age, and pretending otherwise doesn't help anyone. But then you read Joy Clarkson's reflection on love as the most practical force in the Christian life, or Mere Orthodoxy's tender World Down Syndrome Day essay on what it means to be in relation to God, and something shifts. The antidote to fragmentation the tradition keeps pointing us toward isn't a better argument or a stronger institution — it's a deeper account of love, of creaturely dependence, of the irreducible dignity of every person who bears the image of God. That tension — between fracture as sociological diagnosis and love as theological remedy — is worth sitting with.

Today's reading invites a simple but searching question for your own community: are we being formed by patient, embodied love, or by the speed of the moment? The intergenerational discipleship work described here suggests the church has always had an answer to fragmentation — it just requires the long, slow work of showing up for one another across difference. That's not a program. It's a practice.
