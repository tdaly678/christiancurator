Daily Pulse

November 27, 2026

Evangelical writers are pressing this week on what gratitude actually is: not a holiday sentiment, but a theological claim about where reality comes from and who holds it. Scot McKnight's guest Mike Glenn draws a line between gratitude as mood and gratitude as a practiced orientation toward God, the kind that reshapes how a person sees ordinary days. Matthew Westerholm at Crossway goes further back, arguing that creation's rhythms and structures were designed from the beginning to draw Adam into worship, that thankfulness is not a response layered onto experience but woven into how the world works. Robb Brunansky at The Cripplegate grounds this in circumstance: Christians can give thanks in every situation not because things are fine, but because what is true about God does not change with what is true about Tuesday.

Carrie Henry at Christianity Today writes from inside grief, naming the quiet rooms and surfacing memories that make Jehovah Jireh feel less like doctrine and more like a question, and her piece puts real weight on whether the church's theology of provision holds when loss is not resolved. Jake Meador at Mere Orthodoxy pulls the angle outward, arguing with help from Marilynne Robinson and Peter Leithart that gratitude has a public shape, that Thanksgiving is not merely personal but a rehearsal of a community's acknowledgment of dependence. Chris Gehrz takes the same instinct to Mark 8, reading the feeding of the four thousand as a lesson in seeing abundance where scarcity is obvious. That question, raised from different angles earlier in the year, keeps returning: whether Christian formation actually produces people whose perception is changed, who notice grace before they notice its absence. The writers this week do not all agree on where gratitude lives, in liturgy, in doctrine, in practice, in suffering, but they share the conviction that it is not optional and not easy.
