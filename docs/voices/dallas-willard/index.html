Dallas Willard

Philosopher at USC, 1965 to 2012 | Author of The Divine Conspiracy | Wrote on discipleship, spiritual formation and the kingdom of God

About

Dallas Willard (1935 to 2013) was born in Buffalo, Missouri, and taught philosophy at the University of Southern California from 1965 until his retirement at the end of 2012, directing its School of Philosophy from 1982 to 1985. He finished his BA at Baylor University, did his PhD work at the University of Wisconsin, and was ordained as a Baptist minister. His books include The Divine Conspiracy, The Spirit of the Disciplines, Renovation of the Heart and Hearing God, along with translations of and a book on the philosopher Edmund Husserl. He taught in Fuller's DMin program and worked with Gary W. Moon to develop the Renovaré Institute for Spiritual Formation.

Source: About Dallas, Dallas Willard Ministries · Books, Dallas Willard Ministries. Last updated November 12, 2026.

Where to read

Start here

  1. Spiritual Formation: What it is, and How it is Done
    Dallas Willard Ministries

    Defines spiritual formation as the transformation of the heart or will so that Christlike action flows from the whole person, and sets out its progression as vision, intention and method.

  2. The Need, Vision and Strategy of Growing in Christlikeness
    Dallas Willard Ministries · 2006

    Argues that everyone is being formed in some direction and proposes a repeatable path for dropping patterns such as anger, contempt and lust through understanding, intention and practical disciplines.

  3. Rethinking Evangelism
    Dallas Willard Ministries (first published in Cutting Edge, Vineyard USA) · 2001

    Argues that evangelism which reduces salvation to forgiveness of guilt leaves life unchanged, and calls for evangelism that grows out of a community of disciples living under God's reign.

  4. Jesus as Logician and Apologist
    Dallas Willard Ministries · 2006

    Argues that apologetics should be a helping ministry modeled on Jesus, listening to real questions and using simple logic to aid understanding rather than to win arguments.
