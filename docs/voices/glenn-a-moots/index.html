Glenn A. Moots

Professor of political science and philosophy at Northwood University | Author of Politics Reformed | Writes for American Reformer on Protestant political thought and the American founding

About

Glenn A. Moots is Professor and Department Chair of Political Science and Philosophy at Northwood University, where he has taught since 1993. He is the author of Politics Reformed: The Anglo-American Legacy of Covenant Theology (2010) and coeditor, with Phillip Hamilton, of Justifying Revolution: Law, Virtue, and Violence in the American War of Independence (2018). He holds a PhD from Louisiana State University and a BA from the University of Michigan. He writes for American Reformer and Law & Liberty on Protestant political thought, religious liberty, and the American founding.

  1. How Radical Was the American Revolution?
    American Reformer · May 19, 2026

    Adapted from a Hillsdale College lecture, it questions Bernard Bailyn's radical reading of the Revolution and argues that the convictions of colonial Christians, including Jonathan Mayhew's 1750 sermon on resisting tyrants, were an essential cause of independence.

  2. Protestant Constitutionalism and American Independence
    American Reformer · July 9, 2026

    Argues that the Declaration of Independence drew on an older Protestant tradition of resistance, including readings of Romans 13 and the duty of lesser magistrates to restrain a tyrant, rather than on new principles original to Jefferson.

  3. Why Does America Have Religious Liberty?
    American Reformer · April 23, 2024

    Reviews Vincent Phillip Muñoz's Religious Liberty and the American Founding and argues that its natural rights reading credits Jefferson too heavily and leaves out the wider Protestant thinking on church and civil magistrate.

  4. Was the Protestant Reformation a Radical Revolution?
    American Reformer · November 5, 2021

    Responds to critics such as Patrick Deneen and Brad Gregory by arguing that the Magisterial Reformation preserved social order and classical virtue rather than seeding individualism and liberalism.
